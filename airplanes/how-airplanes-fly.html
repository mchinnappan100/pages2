The science

Bernoulli's principle and why wings work

Daniel Bernoulli proved in 1738 that within a flowing fluid, faster-moving regions have lower pressure. A wing exploits this relentlessly.

P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant
P = static pressure  |  ρ = fluid density  |  v = flow velocity
g = gravitational acceleration  |  h = height
Key insight: if v↑ then P↓ — faster air = lower pressure
Why the top of the wing matters

An airfoil (wing cross-section) is curved more on top than the bottom. Air travelling over the curved upper surface must cover a longer path in the same time, so it speeds up. By Bernoulli's theorem, that faster air has lower pressure. The higher pressure below the wing pushes up — that push is lift.

Newton's third law — equally important

The wing is also angled slightly upward (angle of attack). It deflects oncoming air downward. Newton's third law demands an equal and opposite reaction — the air pushes the wing up. Real lift is a combination of both Bernoulli pressure difference and Newtonian momentum change; neither alone is the complete story.

What is an airfoil and how is it designed?

An airfoil is the 2-D cross-sectional shape of a wing. Key parameters:

  • Camber — the asymmetric curvature (top more curved than bottom). More camber = more lift at low speeds.
  • Chord — the straight-line distance from leading edge to trailing edge.
  • Thickness — affects structural strength and high-speed drag.
  • Leading-edge radius — a blunt leading edge tolerates higher angles of attack before stalling.

Early airfoils (NACA 0012) were symmetric. Modern supercritical airfoils (used on widebody jets) have a flatter top to delay the onset of compressibility drag near the speed of sound.

What is angle of attack and what happens at stall?

Angle of attack (AoA) is the angle between the wing chord line and the oncoming airflow. Increasing AoA increases lift — up to a point. At the critical angle of attack (typically 15–20° for a general aviation wing), airflow over the upper surface separates from the wing instead of following its curve. Lift collapses suddenly — this is a stall.

A stall is an aerodynamic event, not an engine failure. An airplane can stall at any speed if AoA is too high. Recovery requires pushing the nose down to reduce AoA and restore smooth airflow.

How does wing sweep change at high speed?

When an aircraft approaches the speed of sound (~343 m/s at sea level), air ahead can no longer "get out of the way" — a shockwave forms. Wing sweep delays this by presenting a thinner effective cross-section to the airflow. A 35° swept wing (like a Boeing 737) experiences Mach effects as if it were flying slower, allowing efficient cruise near Mach 0.85 without full supersonic penalties.

Why is lift proportional to speed squared?

The lift equation is: L = ½ ρ v² S C_L. Because kinetic energy scales with v², doubling airspeed quadruples the dynamic pressure available to generate lift. This is why aircraft can take off with shorter wings at higher speeds, and why slow-flying aircraft (like gliders) need very long, efficient wings.

L = ½ · ρ · v² · S · CL
S = wing area  |  CL = lift coefficient (shape + AoA)
Equilibrium in the sky

The four forces of flight

Every flying aircraft balances four forces. Alter any one and the flight path changes.

Steady level flight

In cruise, all four forces balance exactly: Lift = Weight, Thrust = Drag. The aircraft neither climbs, descends, accelerates, nor decelerates. The pilot's job is to maintain this balance through constantly changing air density, temperature, and weight (as fuel burns).

Three axes of rotation

How pilots steer through three dimensions

An aircraft can rotate around three axes simultaneously. Each axis has its own dedicated control surface.

↔

Roll — ailerons (lateral axis)

Moving the control column/yoke left or right deflects the ailerons — hinged panels at the outer trailing edge of each wing. One aileron goes up (reducing lift), the other goes down (increasing lift). The differential lift rolls the aircraft around its longitudinal axis (nose-to-tail). Used to bank into turns.

↕

Pitch — elevator (lateral axis)

Pulling back on the yoke deflects the elevator — the hinged surface on the horizontal tail — upward. This pushes the tail down and rotates the nose up (increasing angle of attack, producing more lift or climb). Pushing forward does the opposite. On fly-by-wire jets, a side-stick commands pitch directly.

↻

Yaw — rudder (vertical axis)

Foot pedals deflect the rudder — the hinged vertical tail surface. It swings the nose left or right. Rudder is essential for coordinated turns (preventing the skid you'd feel in a car), crosswind landings, and engine-out emergencies on multi-engine aircraft where asymmetric thrust would yaw the plane uncontrollably.

▼

Flaps and slats — high-lift devices

Flaps extend from the inner trailing edge of wings during takeoff and landing. They increase wing area and camber, producing more lift at lower speeds — allowing the aircraft to fly safely below its normal minimum speed. Slats on the leading edge further increase the critical angle of attack before stall. Both are retracted in cruise to reduce drag.

⊗

Spoilers and speedbrakes

Panels that pop up from the upper wing surface to disrupt lift and increase drag. Used to steepen the descent approach without accelerating, and to decelerate rapidly after touchdown (ground spoilers deploy fully, killing lift so the wheels bear the aircraft's full weight and the brakes are maximally effective).
