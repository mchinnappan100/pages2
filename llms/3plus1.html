A framework for frontier AI safety

The governance is necessary. The microscope is missing.

Dario Amodei has proposed three pillars for safer frontier AI — independent evaluation, democratic coordination, and global cooperation. These are essential. But without a fourth pillar — interpretability — we are governing a system we cannot yet see inside.

The urgency

Why safety must keep pace with capability

We should not stop AI progress. We should make sure our ability to understand and control AI progresses alongside it. That is not a restraint on ambition — it is a condition for ambition to be sustainable.

Capability is outrunning understanding

The models we deploy today are extraordinarily capable. But our understanding of their internal mechanisms has not kept pace. We can measure what a model does. We cannot yet explain, from first principles, why it does it.

Behavior is not the whole picture

We can observe inputs and outputs. We can evaluate behavior. We can put guardrails around systems. But what is actually happening inside the model? That question has no satisfying answer yet — and that matters for trust, safety, and control.

The stakes are asymmetric

The cost of a safety failure in a high-stakes system — medical, financial, critical infrastructure — can far exceed the cost of investing in safety research. That asymmetry should change how we allocate attention and resources.

"This is, in my view, the elephant in the room." The three pillars of governance are essential. But governance of a system you cannot see inside is governance with a fundamental blind spot.

The framework

What Dario Amodei proposed

In We Must Pace the Frontier, Anthropic's CEO outlined three concrete steps for making frontier AI development safer. Each addresses a different failure mode. None is redundant.

PILLAR 01
🔍

Independent Evaluators

Give third-party safety evaluators ongoing access to frontier AI companies so they can independently verify safety practices and report incidents.

  • Ongoing access, not one-time audits
  • Independence from the companies being evaluated
  • Public reporting of significant findings
  • Authority to flag incidents before they become crises
An independent panel reviews model behavior under adversarial conditions — attempts to elicit harmful outputs, stress-test edge cases, probe unusual deployment scenarios — and publishes its findings publicly, without the model developer having final editorial control over the results.
PILLAR 02
🤝

Democratic Coordination

Establish common safety standards and coordinate how frontier AI development is managed across countries that share democratic values and governance norms.

  • Shared definitions of high-risk capability
  • Common deployment thresholds and red lines
  • Mutual recognition of evaluation findings
  • Coordinated incident response across borders
A shared definition of "high-risk capability" means that when one country sets a deployment threshold, others can benchmark against it rather than starting from scratch. When an incident occurs in one country, the shared framework allows others to respond quickly rather than waiting for bilateral negotiations.
PILLAR 03
🌐

Global Coordination

Work internationally, including with countries that have very different political systems, to address risks that cannot be contained within national borders or democratic alliances.

  • Addresses risks that cross geopolitical lines
  • Focus on shared existential interests
  • Minimum standards across adversarial relationships
  • Mechanisms for AI-related incident communication
An AI-enabled biological pathogen doesn't stop at borders. Neither does a rogue system optimizing against human interests. Some risks require coordination that goes beyond democratic alliances — the same way nuclear safety required channels of communication even between Cold War adversaries.

The analogy

Operating a powerful machine with no viewport

Consider the boiler. It burns fuel to produce heat. A well-designed boiler has one small but decisive feature that changes everything about operating it safely.

A properly designed boiler has a viewport — a small window through which the operator can see the flame directly. Is it blue? Is it yellow? Is something abnormal happening? That simple opening gives the operator something extraordinarily valuable: visibility into the process happening inside the machine.

Now imagine operating a powerful boiler with no viewport at all. You only know that water is getting hot. You can measure the temperature. You can measure the pressure. You can install safety valves. You can have inspectors walk the floor and test the outputs. But you cannot see the flame.

That is roughly where we are with frontier AI.

BOILER WITH VIEWPORT

What the operator can do

  • Measure temperature and pressure
  • See the color and behavior of the flame directly
  • Detect abnormality before it becomes failure
  • Understand why a reading changed, not just that it changed
  • Intervene precisely, not just reactively

BOILER WITHOUT VIEWPORT

What today's AI operator can do

  • Measure inputs and outputs
  • Evaluate behavioral benchmarks
  • Install guardrails and safety valves
  • Hire inspectors to probe the system externally
  • Not see what is actually happening inside

Transformer models don't have a conventional program where we can simply inspect the source code and understand why a particular behavior occurred. Researchers are making significant progress in mechanistic interpretability and circuit analysis. But we are still far from having a complete internal map of a frontier model.

We have increasingly sophisticated ways to measure behavior, evaluate models, and impose safeguards. But none of that is the same as seeing the flame. None of that is the viewport.

We have the rules, the inspectors, and the safety valves. What we are missing is the window into the process itself — and that window is what interpretability is trying to build.

The addition

Interpretability: build the microscope

If we can understand which internal circuits produce a particular behavior, we move from simply observing what an AI does toward understanding why it does it. That changes the safety equation fundamentally.

Neural circuits and representations

Map the internal structures that encode concepts, relationships, and reasoning paths within the model — the basic anatomy before we can understand the physiology.

Mechanistic interpretability

Understand how specific computations are implemented at the level of weights and activations — not just what a model does, but how it does it at the mechanistic level.

Causal analysis of model behavior

Move from correlation (the model outputs X when input Y) to causation (input Y caused output X because of internal mechanism Z) — the difference between observation and understanding.

Detection of deceptive strategies

Develop tools that can identify when a model is pursuing a strategy that differs from the one it appears to be pursuing externally — the capability that makes independent evaluation far more powerful.

Tracing representations to actions

Build methods for tracing how internal representations flow through the model and give rise to specific outputs — the chain of causation from thought to action, made visible.

Real-time internal monitoring

Create tools that allow important internal mechanisms to be observed while the model is running — not just after the fact — so that inspectors have something real to inspect.

The stronger and more defensible argument is not that interpretability will predict every rogue action before it happens. It is that greater causal understanding could substantially improve our ability to detect, explain, evaluate, and potentially control dangerous behavior.

The complete picture

Complementary layers, not competing approaches

These four pillars are not in tension with each other. Each addresses a failure mode the others cannot cover. Together they form a safety infrastructure where external accountability is grounded in internal understanding.

01

Governance tells us what standards we should follow — the rules that define what responsible development looks like.

02

Independent evaluators tell us whether those standards are actually being followed — the inspectors who can verify claims that companies make about their own systems.

03

International coordination gives us a way to address risks that cross borders — the channel that makes safety a shared challenge rather than a competitive one.

04

Interpretability gives us the microscope to understand what is happening inside the system — the instrument that makes the first three pillars far more powerful, because inspectors with a viewport are fundamentally different from inspectors without one.

05

The first three pillars give us the rules, the inspectors, and the coordination. The fourth pillar gives us the viewport. Without it, even the best inspectors are reading a boiler with no window.

06

This is not an argument against Amodei's framework. It is an argument that the framework needs a scientific instrument underneath it — one that turns governance from behavioral observation into causal understanding.

07

The objective is not to brake every form of AI research. Quite the opposite — we should accelerate AI research, and accelerate AI safety research even faster. The instrument has to keep up with what it is being asked to measure.

The engineering principle

There is an old engineering principle: if you can't measure it, you can't control it. For increasingly autonomous AI systems, measurement cannot stop at the input and output. We need better instruments for looking inside. The boiler that gets built right is the one with a viewport.
