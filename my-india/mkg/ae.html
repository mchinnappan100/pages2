A Tribute to Genius

Albert Einstein

Theoretical Physicist  ·  Nobel Laureate  ·  Humanist

✦   ✦   ✦

To the man who bent the fabric of space and time with nothing but thought — who showed that imagination is the highest form of intelligence, and that a single curious mind can illuminate the darkness of the unknown.

Albert Einstein, born into an ordinary family in Württemberg, Germany, grew into the most celebrated scientific mind of the twentieth century. His general theory of relativity rewrote the laws of the cosmos; his Nobel Prize–winning explanation of the photoelectric effect founded quantum physics. Yet he remained, at heart, a man of profound moral courage — a refugee, a pacifist, a conscience of humanity in its most dangerous hour.

This page is offered in reverence to his curiosity, his courage, and the undying flame of scientific wonder he carried from the patent office in Bern to the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton — and into the hearts of all who dare to question.

◈ Pillars of His Thought

⚡

Special Relativity

Space and time are relative to the observer's motion — simultaneity is not absolute, and the speed of light is the universe's ultimate constant.

🌌

General Relativity

Gravity is not a force but a curvature of spacetime caused by mass — predicting black holes, gravitational waves, and the expansion of the cosmos.

⚛

E = mc²

Mass and energy are interchangeable — a relationship that underpins nuclear energy, particle physics, and our deepest understanding of matter.

💡

Photoelectric Effect

Light behaves as discrete packets (photons) — the insight that founded quantum physics and earned Einstein the 1921 Nobel Prize.

☮

Pacifism

A lifelong commitment to non-violence and international cooperation, culminating in the 1955 Russell-Einstein Manifesto warning against nuclear war.

🔭

Curiosity

Einstein regarded relentless questioning as the engine of all discovery — "the most important thing is not to stop questioning."

◈ Milestones of a Great Life

1879

Birth in Ulm

Born 14 March into a secular Jewish family in the Kingdom of Württemberg, Germany.

1895

ETH Zurich

Sat the entrance examination for the Swiss Federal Polytechnic, beginning his formal scientific education.

1900

Teaching Diploma

Graduated from ETH Zurich with a teaching diploma in mathematics and physics.

1901

Swiss Citizenship

Became a Swiss citizen after renouncing his German citizenship in 1896; began work at the Bern Patent Office.

1905

Annus Mirabilis

Published four landmark papers — on the photoelectric effect, Brownian motion, special relativity, and E=mc² — transforming physics in a single year.

1915

General Relativity

Presented the complete general theory of relativity, describing gravity as the curvature of spacetime.

1919

Eclipse Confirms Relativity

Eddington's solar eclipse observations confirmed the bending of starlight by gravity, making Einstein a global celebrity overnight.

1921

Nobel Prize in Physics

Awarded the Nobel Prize for the discovery of the photoelectric effect — the foundational act of quantum physics.

1933

Emigration to Princeton

Fled Nazi Germany and joined the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, New Jersey, never to return to Europe.

1955

Death in Princeton

Died on 18 April, aged 76. His final act: co-authoring the Russell-Einstein Manifesto urging nations to abandon nuclear weapons.

◈ An Immortal Legacy

Albert Einstein's theories fundamentally reshaped our understanding of the physical universe. His general theory of relativity predicted phenomena — black holes, gravitational waves, the expansion of the cosmos — that were not confirmed until decades after his death. Technologies as everyday as GPS satellites depend on relativistic corrections he first calculated at a desk in Bern.

Beyond physics, Einstein was a moral voice of the twentieth century. As a Jewish refugee who had fled Nazi persecution, he understood oppression firsthand. He campaigned for civil rights, warned of the dangers of nuclear weapons, and believed that the scientist's responsibility to humanity does not end at the laboratory door.

The United Nations declared 14 March — his birthday — World Pi Day and, separately, Albert Einstein Day. A chemical element, Einsteinium (99), bears his name. Time magazine named him Person of the Century in 1999. Yet his truest monument is not carved in stone or written in textbooks: it is every child who looks at the stars and wonders why.

"The important thing is not to stop questioning." He lived those six words completely — and that question still echoes.
