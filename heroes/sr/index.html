01 / The beginning

A boy who saw magic in numbers.

Srinivasa Ramanujan was born on 22 December 1887 in Erode, in what was then the Madras Presidency of British India. He grew up in Kumbakonam, Tamil Nadu.

From a young age, mathematics fascinated him. Numbers, equations and infinite series became his world. He explored mathematical ideas with extraordinary curiosity.

A mathematics book by G. S. Carr, containing thousands of mathematical results, became a turning point in his life. He studied its contents independently and began developing remarkable results of his own.

His academic journey was not easy. His intense concentration on mathematics affected his performance in other subjects, and he lost college scholarships.

He had little formal training in advanced mathematics, but an extraordinary ability to discover mathematical truths.

His notebooks began filling with formulas, identities, theorems and mathematical discoveries.

03 / A letter that changed history

A letter to Cambridge.

In January 1913, Ramanujan wrote to the distinguished British mathematician G. H. Hardy at the University of Cambridge.

The letter contained a remarkable collection of mathematical formulas and theorems. Hardy initially examined them with his colleague J. E. Littlewood.

They soon recognised that the work came from an extraordinary mathematical mind.

Hardy invited Ramanujan to Cambridge. In 1914, Ramanujan travelled from India to England, beginning one of the most remarkable collaborations in mathematical history.

Two extraordinary minds. One shared passion. A partnership that enriched mathematics forever.

Together, Hardy and Ramanujan worked on problems that included the mathematics of integer partitions.

04 / The famous taxi number
1729

An ordinary number? Never!

One of the most beloved stories in mathematics is about a taxi, a hospital visit and Ramanujan's extraordinary insight.

Hardy once visited Ramanujan in hospital and mentioned that the taxi he had taken had the rather uninteresting number 1729. Ramanujan immediately recognised its extraordinary mathematical property.

1³ + 12³ = 1729
9³ + 10³ = 1729

The smallest positive integer expressible as the sum of two positive cubes in two different ways!

This number is now famously known as the Hardy–Ramanujan number.
