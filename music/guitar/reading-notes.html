A player's field guide

Guitar music is written two different ways. Here's how to read both.

Standard notation tells you pitch and rhythm on a five-line staff — the same system every instrument uses. Tablature tells you exactly where to put your fingers — six lines for six strings, numbers for frets. Most guitarists end up reading a mix of both. This guide covers each system, then ties them together on the fretboard.

System one

Standard notation on the staff

Guitar music uses the treble clef — even though the guitar actually sounds a full octave lower than written. This is a convention that keeps guitar parts from needing constant ledger lines below the staff.

Open strings, low to high, mapped onto the staff

The two building blocks

Lines (bottom to top): E · G · B · D · F — remembered with "Every Good Boy Does Fine."

Spaces (bottom to top): F · A · C · E — spells "FACE."

Why guitar needs ledger lines

The guitar's low E string sits two ledger lines below the staff, and high notes on the top strings often float several lines above it — guitar has one of the widest playable ranges of any common instrument, so ledger lines show up constantly once you're past open position.

Sharps, flats, and the key signature

A ♯ (sharp) raises a note one fret/semitone; a ♭ (flat) lowers it one. Symbols placed at the start of the staff (the key signature) apply for the whole line unless cancelled by a ♮ (natural) sign next to a note.

System two

Tablature (tab)

Tab was built specifically for fretted instruments. It skips pitch names entirely and tells you directly where to place your fingers.

1

Six lines = six strings

The bottom line is your low E string, the top line is your high e string — the tab staff is drawn as if you're looking down at your own guitar neck.

2

Numbers = frets

A number on a line tells you which fret to press on that string. 0 means play the string open — don't press anything.

3

Read left to right

Numbers stacked directly on top of each other are played together as a chord. Numbers spread out left to right are played one after another as a melody.

A single-note phrase, then a chord (numbers stacked)
What tab doesn't tell you

Standard tab shows pitch and finger position, but not always rhythm. That's why tab is often paired with standard notation above it, or with rhythm slashes — the tab tells you where, the notation or slashes tell you when and for how long.

Where the two systems meet

The fretboard, mapped out

Every fret on every string is a specific note name. Once this grid is memorized, you can translate freely between standard notation, tab, and the neck in front of you.

Strings low E (bottom) to high e (top) · frets 0–12
The one shortcut worth memorizing

The 12th fret is the same note as the open string, one octave higher — the whole pattern below it just repeats. Learn frets 0–12 well and you effectively know the entire neck.

The "when," not just the "where"

Reading rhythm

Note shapes tell you how long to hold a pitch, measured in beats. This works identically whether the pitch itself comes from standard notation or from a tab number.

Put it together

Reading a line, step by step

Here's the same short phrase written as tab. Walk through it left to right using the fretboard grid above to check yourself.

Walk through it
  1. First number is on the low E string (bottom line) at fret 3 — that's a G.
  2. Next is the A string (second from bottom) at fret 2 — that's a B.
  3. Then the D string open (fret 0) — a D.
  4. Then the G string at fret 2 — an A.
  5. The last group has three numbers stacked — play all three strings together as a chord.
