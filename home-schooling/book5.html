🏆 Halfway Milestone — Book 5 of 10!

Welcome to Book 5: Our Earth, Our Sky & Reading Takes Off! — the midpoint of the series and the book where the world gets dramatically bigger. We zoom from the neighborhood and community of Book 3 all the way out to continents, oceans, planets, and stars.

This is also the book where reading transitions from sounding out words to reading for meaning and pleasure. By the end of Unit 4, your child will read a full multi-sentence passage independently — a life-changing milestone that opens every library, every subject, and every dream to them.

★How to Use This Book

💡 Big picture thinking begins here. Units 1–3 deal with the largest scales a child can imagine. Use a globe or world map throughout — having a physical globe to spin and point to makes geography visceral and memorable.
  1. Get a globe or print a world map — keep it visible during Units 1, 2, and 3. Point to where you live on every first day of each unit.
  2. For Unit 3, go outside on a clear night and look at the moon and stars together before the lesson. The experience anchors the knowledge.
  3. For Unit 4, choose one real picture book to read together start-to-finish this week — full book comprehension is the goal, not just individual passages.
  4. For Unit 5, gather base-ten manipulatives: bundles of 10 sticks (popsicle sticks bundled with rubber bands) work perfectly and cost almost nothing.
  5. For Unit 6, establish a "measuring station" with a ruler, a balance scale (or a coat hanger with bags), and measuring cups.

★Learning Goals

  • Name the 7 continents and 5 oceans and locate them on a globe or map
  • Describe the four seasons and explain what causes day and night
  • Name the 8 planets in order from the sun
  • Read a 5–8 sentence passage aloud with fluency and answer comprehension questions
  • Understand place value — identify the tens and ones in any two-digit number up to 99
  • Measure length with non-standard units; compare weights; compare volumes
  • Locate their home country on a world map and name their continent

🌍 Unit 1 — Earth: Land & Water

Lesson for Kids

📖 Story Time: The Blue and Green Planet

When the first astronauts flew to the moon and looked back, they saw something that took their breath away: a small, beautiful ball floating in the blackness of space — blue and green and white, swirled with clouds.

"It looked like a marble," one astronaut said. "A perfect blue marble."

That marble is our Earth — the only planet we know of with liquid water, with air to breathe, with life everywhere you look.

From space, Earth looks mostly blue — because about 71% of its surface is covered by water: five great oceans. The green and brown patches are the land — seven huge pieces of land called continents.

Mia got a globe and slowly spun it. She found the big piece that said North America — that's where she lived. She found Africa, Asia, Europe, South America, Australia, and Antarctica at the very bottom, covered in ice.

"It's one planet," she whispered. "But it's so BIG."

"And every single person who has ever lived," said her mom, "has lived on this one marble. We're all neighbors."

Activity 1 — Globe Explorer

Spin a globe and stop it with one finger. Ask: "Is your finger on land or water?" Keep score over 20 spins. You'll find water wins about 7 times out of 10 — a hands-on discovery of Earth's 71% water coverage.

Activity 2 — Salt Dough Landforms

Mix 2 cups flour, 1 cup salt, 1 cup water. Use this dough to sculpt a mountain, a river valley, and a flat plain on a tray. Let dry overnight. Paint blue for water, green for land, brown for mountains.

Activity 3 — Continent Song

Sing to the tune of "Are You Sleeping?": "North America, South America, Europe too, Africa and Asia, Australia, Antarctica — seven in all, seven in all!" Repeat daily until memorized.

Parent Guide — Unit 1: Earth, Land & Water

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Why world geography at age 4–5: Geographic knowledge builds global awareness, contextualizes history, and develops the spatial reasoning that supports mathematics. Children who learn continents and oceans early carry an organizational mental map that makes every future social studies lesson click into place faster.

  1. Always orient to home first: "We live in [city], which is in [state], which is in the United States of America, which is in North America." Zoom out from the known to the unknown.
  2. Keep the globe accessible — let your child spin it and explore freely outside lesson time. Curiosity-driven exploration covers more ground than instruction alone.
  3. Connect continents to things the child knows: "Elephants live in Africa and Asia. Polar bears live near the Arctic Ocean."
  4. The salt dough activity is messy and wonderful — the act of sculpting a mountain makes it far more memorable than looking at a picture of one.
✅ Seven continents memory trick: "Eat An Apple As A Nice Snack" — Europe, Antarctica, Asia, Africa, Australia, North America, South America.

Assessment — Unit 1: Earth

🌍 Globe Quiz (10 minutes)

Q1. Point to each continent on the globe. Ask: "What is this continent called?" for all seven.
Q2. Ask: "Which continent do we live on?" and "Name one other continent."
Q3. Ask: "What is an ocean? Can you name one ocean?"
Q4. Show a picture of a mountain and a desert. Ask: "What is this called? What is it like there?"

⛅ Unit 2 — Weather & Seasons

Lesson for Kids

📖 Story Time: Why Does Weather Change?

Nora used to think weather just happened randomly — sunny one day, rainy the next, for no reason at all.

But then her teacher explained: "Weather is the air moving and changing. When the sun heats the ground, warm air rises. Cooler air rushes in to take its place. That moving air is called WIND."

"And rain?" asked Nora.

"Water from oceans and lakes evaporates — turns into invisible vapor — floats up high, cools down, and forms clouds. When the clouds get heavy enough with tiny water droplets, they fall as RAIN. Or if it's cold enough — SNOW."

Nora stepped outside. She felt the wind on her face. She looked at a cloud. "That cloud came from the ocean?"

"Maybe from an ocean miles away. Water travels the whole planet in a cycle — evaporate, cloud, rain, river, ocean, evaporate again. It never stops."

Nora looked at a puddle on the ground. "So this puddle could become a cloud someday?"

"Yes," said her teacher. "And the rain in that cloud might fall on a different continent. Water is the great traveler."

Nora stepped deliberately into the puddle. "Bon voyage," she said to the water.

The Water Cycle (in 4 steps)

StepWhat HappensWhere You See It
1. EvaporationSun heats water; water turns to vapor and risesPuddles disappear on a sunny day
2. CondensationWater vapor cools high up; forms tiny droplets → cloudsClouds in the sky; fog on cold mornings
3. PrecipitationDroplets combine, get heavy, fall as rain or snowRain, snow, hail, sleet

Activity 1 — Mini Water Cycle in a Bag

Fill a zip-lock bag one-quarter full with water. Add a few drops of blue food coloring. Tape it to a sunny window. Over 1–2 days, watch condensation form at the top, "rain" back down. This is evaporation and condensation in miniature — one of the most stunning science activities for this age.

Activity 2 — Season Scrapbook

Make a 4-page season book: one page per season. Draw or glue pictures of typical weather, clothing, activities, and plants/animals for each season. Discuss: "What season is it right now? What season comes next?"

Activity 3 — Weather Reporter

Each morning for two weeks, your child is the "weather reporter." They look outside, describe the weather, and record it on a simple chart (sunny ☀️ / cloudy ☛⛅ / rainy 🌧️ / windy 💨 / snowy ❄️). At the end of two weeks, count each type. Graph the results with sticker dots.

💡 Day and night science: Explain that day and night happen because Earth SPINS — not because the sun moves. Roll a ball in front of a flashlight to demonstrate. The side facing the flashlight = day; the side facing away = night.

Parent Guide — Unit 2: Weather & Seasons

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Why weather science at age 4–5: The water cycle is one of the most important systems on Earth — it connects oceans, atmosphere, land, and all living things. Teaching it early gives children a mental model of how natural systems work: cyclically, interdependently, and globally.

  1. The zip-lock bag activity is deeply memorable — set it up and let the child observe and document it over two days.
  2. Connect seasons to the child's personal experience: birthdays in summer or winter, holidays in different seasons, clothes that change.
  3. Introduce the concept of Earth's tilt causing seasons: "We have summer when our part of Earth is tilted toward the sun. Winter when we're tilted away." A flashlight and a tilted ball demonstrates this beautifully.
  4. The weather reporter activity over two weeks builds data collection habits — a foundational science skill — alongside graph-reading (a math skill).
🔭 Day/night preview: This unit bridges naturally into Unit 3 (solar system) — day and night are caused by Earth's rotation, and seasons are caused by Earth's orbit and tilt. Connect them explicitly as you move between units.

Assessment — Unit 2: Weather & Seasons

⛅ Weather & Seasons Check (10 minutes)

Q1. Ask: "Name the four seasons in order, starting from spring."
Q2. Ask: "What is happening inside our zip-lock bag? Why does water appear at the top?"
Q3. Ask: "Why is it daytime for us right now? Where is night-time happening?" Use a flashlight and globe to test understanding.
Q4. Review the 2-week weather chart. Ask: "Which type of weather happened most? Which happened least? What season do you think produced this weather?"

🪐 Unit 3 — The Solar System

Lesson for Kids

📖 Story Time: Eight Planets and One Amazing Star

Far, far away — so far that even the fastest airplane would take millions of years to get there — there are other worlds.

Our solar system has one star — the Sun — and eight planets orbiting around it, each on their own path, spinning through space like a giant cosmic dance.

Closest to the sun: tiny, hot Mercury. Then Venus, blazing hot with thick yellow clouds. Then our home — beautiful Earth, third from the sun, just the right distance for life.

Next comes red Mars — scientists are looking for signs of ancient life there right now! Then giant Jupiter, so big you could fit 1,300 Earths inside it, with a storm bigger than our whole planet that has been raging for 350 years.

Then Saturn with its magnificent rings made of ice and rock. Then Uranus, spinning on its side as if it tripped and never got back up. And farthest out, cold dark Neptune, where winds blow at 1,200 miles per hour.

Leo lay on the grass and looked up at the night sky. All those stars — each one was a sun, possibly with its own planets, possibly with its own children lying in the grass looking up.

"The universe is really, really big," he told his dad.

"Yes," said his dad. "And you are part of it."

🟡VenusHottest overall; thick clouds
🔵EarthOur home — life!
🔴MarsRed; has two moons

Activity 1 — Solar System Mobile

Cut 8 circles of different sizes from cardstock. Color them: Mercury (grey), Venus (yellow), Earth (blue/green), Mars (red), Jupiter (orange/brown stripes), Saturn (yellow with a ring cut from paper), Uranus (light blue), Neptune (dark blue). Hang them on strings from a coat hanger in order.

Activity 2 — Scale Walk

Demonstrate scale: put a basketball as the Sun at one end of the yard. Mercury is a peppercorn 10 steps away. Earth is a marble 26 steps away. Jupiter is a golf ball 135 steps away. The distances are staggering — this makes the concept of "space" real in a way no picture can.

Activity 3 — Moon Phases Journal

For two weeks, go outside at night (or look through a window) and draw the shape of the moon each night. Label each drawing with the date. Watch it go from crescent to half to full and back again — a two-week science experiment that happens in the sky every month.

🔭 Big idea for young minds: The Sun is a star — the same as those tiny dots we see at night, just much closer. Every star in the night sky is a sun, often bigger than ours. There are more stars in the universe than grains of sand on all of Earth's beaches. This is true. And it is astonishing.

Parent Guide — Unit 3: Solar System

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Why space at age 4–5: Astronomy produces wonder — and wonder is the emotional engine of all learning. Children who are given the true, magnificent scale of the universe early develop a deep curiosity and a healthy sense of perspective that informs their relationship with knowledge for life. Do not water this down. Give them the real facts, even the staggering ones.

  1. Use the memory sentence (My Very Excited Mother Just Served Us Nachos) to lock in the order of the planets — it's still the first thing most adults do when asked to list the planets.
  2. The scale walk is transformative — do it even if it takes 30 minutes. Children who walk the distances never forget them.
  3. Start the moon phases journal immediately — it takes two weeks and the payoff is a complete scientific record the child made themselves.
  4. Connect to Unit 2: Earth orbits the sun once a year (that's what makes a year!), and Earth spins once a day (that's what makes a day and night). These connections build a coherent mental model of why time works the way it does.
✅ Note on Pluto: Pluto was reclassified as a "dwarf planet" in 2006. You can acknowledge it as a beloved former-planet: "Pluto is a dwarf planet — smaller than our Moon, very far away, and very cold. Scientists gave it a new category."

Assessment — Unit 3: Solar System

🪐 Planet Explorer Check (10 minutes)

Q1. Point to each planet on the mobile. Ask: "What is this planet's name? What do you know about it?"
Q2. Ask: "Which planet is closest to the sun? Which is farthest?"
Q3. Ask: "Why does it get dark at night? Why do we have a daytime and a nighttime every day?" (Earth spinning.)
Q4. Show the moon phases journal. Ask: "What shape was the moon on [date]? What shape was it a week later? What do you think happens after a full moon?"

📖 Unit 4 — Reading Takes Off!

Lesson for Kids

📖 Story Time: The Day Reading Became Real

Before she learned to read, books were just pictures with mysterious marks underneath. Then she learned the sounds. Then she learned to blend. Then came sentences.

And one day — she picked up a book, and she read it. Not one word. Not one sentence. The whole book.

It was only eight pages long and had big pictures, but she read every word on every page herself, and when she was done, she knew what happened: a little duck had gotten lost and then found its way home.

She sat very still for a moment. Then she went to find her mom.

"I read a book," she said. "A whole book."

Her mom smiled and didn't say anything — she just held her daughter close. Because some moments don't need words. They only need to be felt.

After a while the girl pulled away. "Do we have more books?" she asked.

Reading Passage Practice

Read the following passage together. Point to each word. The child reads; you support only when they get stuck. Then answer the comprehension questions together.

The Big Red Hen

The hen sat on the mat. She had six eggs. The eggs were big and tan. One egg cracked. A chick came out! It was wet and soft. The hen sat still. She kept the chick warm. The chick grew and grew. Now the chick is big. Now the chick is a hen too!

Comprehension Questions

📖 After Reading — Let's Talk!

Q1. "Where did the hen sit? What did she have with her?" (Recalls detail.)
Q2. "What happened to one of the eggs? What came out?" (Sequences events.)
Q3. "Why did the hen sit still and keep the chick warm?" (Infers motivation.)
Q4. "What do you think the chick will do when it grows up and has its own eggs?" (Predicts — the highest comprehension skill.)

Activity 1 — Read a Real Book This Week

Choose one easy reader book from the library (level: Pre-K or Kindergarten). Read it together every day this week — a different child-led session each time. By the fifth reading, your child should be reading most of it independently. Fluency comes from repetition with familiar text.

Activity 2 — Build a Sentence

Write 6 words on separate cards: The / hen / sat / on / the / mat. Scramble them. Ask your child to put them back in order. Read the result. Then swap one word: replace "sat" with "ran" — read the new sentence. This builds flexible sentence construction.

Activity 3 — Author's Chair

Your child dictates a 4–6 sentence story; you write it exactly as they say it. Read it back together. Then ask: "What would you like to change? What could happen next?" This is collaborative writing — it treats the child as a real author and develops narrative voice.

✅ Reading fluency milestones for age 4–5: CVC words (Books 3–4) → simple sentences (Book 4) → short passages with comprehension (Book 5) → reading simple books independently (Book 6–7). Every child is on their own timeline — celebrate progress, not speed.

Parent Guide — Unit 4: Reading Takes Off

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Moving from decoding to comprehension: Decoding (sounding out words) and reading comprehension are two different skills that must both be developed. Many children who decode fluently struggle to answer questions about what they read, and vice versa. This unit explicitly develops both in parallel.

  1. Always ask comprehension questions after every reading — not just "Did you like it?" but "What happened? Why did the character do that? What do you think will happen next?"
  2. The four question types in the assessment (recall, sequence, infer, predict) mirror the four levels of reading comprehension tested all the way through high school. Building these habits at age 4 is a profound head start.
  3. Re-reading the same book multiple times builds fluency — resist the urge to always introduce new books. Familiarity allows the child to focus on expression and meaning rather than decoding.
  4. Your local library's children's librarian is an invaluable resource — they can recommend exactly the right level books for your child's current reading ability.
❤️ The most important reading habit: Read aloud to your child every day, even after they can read independently. Children who are read to at a level above their own reading ability develop vocabulary, syntax, and comprehension far beyond their decoding level. This gap — between what they can read and what they can understand when read to — is the space where intelligence grows.

Assessment — Unit 4: Reading

📖 Reading Fluency & Comprehension Check

Q1. Ask the child to read the "Big Red Hen" passage independently. Note: fluency (smooth vs. choppy), accuracy (correct words), and expression (does it sound like language or just letter sounds?).
Q2. Ask the four comprehension questions above. Note which levels (recall, sequence, infer, predict) the child handles confidently.
Q3. Give an unfamiliar short passage (3–4 sentences). Read it once together, then ask: "What was that about?" — Tests transfer to new texts.
Q4. Ask the child to read one page from the library book independently. Celebrate every word they get right.

🔟 Unit 5 — Place Value to 99

Lesson for Kids

📖 Story Time: The Tens and Ones Secret

Marco had 47 pennies in a jar. Counting them one by one took FOREVER.

"There's a faster way," said his grandma. She helped him group the pennies into groups of 10. They made 4 groups of 10 — and had 7 left over.

"Four groups of TEN," said Grandma, "and SEVEN ones. That's forty-seven! See how the 4 in 47 means FOUR TENS? And the 7 means SEVEN ONES?"

Marco stared at the number 47 like he was seeing it for the first time. "The first number tells me how many groups of 10 I have?"

"Exactly. That's called PLACE VALUE. Every number has a TENS place and a ONES place. They're like two different zip codes in the same number."

Marco grabbed a piece of paper and wrote 47. He drew a line between the digits: 4 | 7. "Four tens. Seven ones."

He looked at the number 83. "Eight tens and three ones — eighty-three!"

"You've just unlocked the secret of every number from 10 to 99," said Grandma. "And once you understand that, bigger numbers — hundreds, thousands, millions — are just more of the same idea."

Marco counted his pennies again. It took about 10 seconds. "Place value is amazing," he decided.

The Place Value Structure

4TENS place
7ONES place
= 47Forty-seven

Activity 1 — Bundle and Count

Collect 60 popsicle sticks. Rubber-band them into groups of 10. To show 34: put out 3 bundles of 10 and 4 loose sticks. Say: "3 tens and 4 ones = thirty-four." Have the child build numbers you call out, and call out numbers they build.

NumberTensOnesSay It
2323Twenty-three
4747Forty-seven
6060Sixty
8585Eighty-five
9999Ninety-nine

Activity 2 — Place Value Houses

Draw two houses side by side on paper: label the left house "TENS" and the right house "ONES." Write a two-digit number below. The child writes the tens digit in the tens house and the ones digit in the ones house. Make it visual and spatial.

Activity 3 — Who Has More?

Write two two-digit numbers (e.g., 52 and 35). Build both with bundles. Ask: "Which is more? How do you know without counting every single one?" Guide toward the insight: compare the tens first — the number with more tens is always larger (when tens are equal, compare the ones).

✅ Why place value is the most important math concept in early childhood: Every two, three, four, and five-digit arithmetic operation — addition, subtraction, multiplication, long division — depends entirely on understanding place value. This is the master key to all of elementary arithmetic.

Parent Guide — Unit 5: Place Value

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Place value — the cornerstone of arithmetic: Research consistently shows that children who deeply understand place value (not just the procedure, but the meaning of each digit's position) dramatically outperform peers in all arithmetic operations through middle school. This unit is among the most mathematically important in the entire series.

  1. Always use physical bundles first. The abstract (writing the number) must follow the concrete (holding the bundles).
  2. When reading a two-digit number together, always say the full value: "The 4 means 4 TENS, which is forty." Do not just say "four-seven" — say "forty-seven." This builds the connection between digits and values.
  3. Connect to counting: count by tens together (10, 20, 30… 90) — this is skip-counting and it mirrors the tens structure of place value perfectly.
  4. The "who has more" comparison activity seeds the comparison algorithm used through algebra: compare the highest-value digit first, then move right.
⚠️ A common confusion: Children often think the number 23 is "bigger" than 19 because 3 is bigger than 9. Correct this by always building the numbers physically first — the bundles make it visually obvious that 2 tens beats 1 ten regardless of the ones.

Assessment — Unit 5: Place Value

🔟 Place Value Detective (10 minutes)

Q1. Build 56 with bundles. Ask: "How many tens? How many ones? What number is this?"
Q2. Write the number 73. Ask: "What does the 7 mean? What does the 3 mean?"
Q3. Give two numbers: 48 and 62. Ask: "Which is larger? How do you know?" Observe whether they compare tens first or count every single bundle.
Q4. Count by tens together from 10 to 90. Ask: "What pattern do you notice? What comes after 90 when we count by tens?" (100 — a preview of three-digit numbers!)

📏 Unit 6 — Measurement

Lesson for Kids

📖 Story Time: How Big Is Big?

Everyone in the village wanted to know: how tall was the great oak tree?

The baker said it was 20 loaves of bread tall. The carpenter said it was 12 ladders tall. The child said it was 100 of her hand-spans tall.

They were all correct — but none of them could compare answers, because they'd each used a different measuring unit!

"This," said the wise teacher, "is why humans invented standard measurements — so that everyone uses the same units and can share information."

An inch is always the same length anywhere in the world. A pound always weighs the same. A cup always holds the same amount of liquid. Standard units are how people who have never met can share information.

The child looked at her hands. "My hand-span is my own personal ruler," she said, "but a real ruler measures the same for everyone."

"Exactly," said the teacher. "But starting with your hand-span is a perfectly wonderful way to begin."

Three Types of Measurement

TypeWhat We MeasureNon-Standard UnitStandard Unit
📏 LengthHow long or tall something isHand-spans, paper clips, crayonsInches, feet, centimeters
⚖️ WeightHow heavy something isCubes on a balance, "heavier/lighter"Pounds, ounces, grams
🫙 VolumeHow much liquid or space something holdsSpoonfuls, small cups, scoopsCups, pints, liters

Activity 1 — Measure with Non-Standard Units

Choose 5 objects around the house (a book, a shoe, the kitchen table, a pillow, a window). Measure each one using paper clips laid end-to-end. Record: "The book is 9 paper clips long." Then re-measure one object with a different unit (crayons). Discuss: "Why did we get a different number?"

Activity 2 — Balance Scale Comparisons

Make a simple balance scale from a coat hanger, string, and two paper cups. Hang it from a doorknob. Compare pairs of objects: apple vs. orange, block vs. feather, shoe vs. sock. Record each comparison: "The apple is heavier than the orange."

Activity 3 — Volume Exploration

Fill a large plastic container with water. Provide cups of different sizes. Ask: "How many small scoops does it take to fill the medium cup? How many medium cups to fill the large container?" This builds multiplicative thinking — a foundation for multiplication in later years.

💡 Introduce the ruler: Show a ruler and explain that each number marks one inch. Measure the same objects from Activity 1 with the ruler. Compare the ruler result to the paper-clip result. Ask: "Which measurement can we share with someone far away — paper clips or inches? Why?"

Parent Guide — Unit 6: Measurement

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Why measurement at age 4–5: Measurement connects mathematics to the physical world. It builds number sense (understanding that 12 inches = 1 foot requires multiplicative reasoning), vocabulary (longer, shorter, heavier, lighter, more, less), and scientific thinking (precise observation). Every science experiment and cooking recipe in their future will require measurement literacy.

  1. Non-standard units before standard units — always. The conceptual understanding (longer objects need more units to measure) must precede the memorization of inch or centimeter values.
  2. Cook together as a measurement activity — doubling a recipe, filling measuring cups, and weighing ingredients is applied measurement in a context children love.
  3. Introduce the language of comparison constantly: "The dog is taller than the cat. The couch is wider than the door. Which is longer?"
  4. Connect to number sense: "The book is 9 paper clips long. If we used bigger paper clips, would the number be bigger or smaller?" This question reveals deep understanding.
✅ US measurement preview: Introduce inch and foot as names at this stage. Full conversion fluency (12 inches = 1 foot, 3 feet = 1 yard) is a 1st–2nd grade milestone — plant the seed now with the ruler, harvest it later.

Assessment — Unit 6: Measurement

📏 Measurement Explorer Check (10 minutes)

Q1. Place two objects of clearly different lengths. Ask: "Which is longer? How do you know?" Then ask the child to measure both with paper clips and report the number.
Q2. Balance two objects. Ask: "Which is heavier? How does the scale show you that?"
Q3. Show a ruler. Ask: "What is this? What does it measure? Can you measure this pencil with it?"
Q4. Pour water into two different shaped containers (tall thin vs. short wide). Ask: "Which has more water?" Then pour both into measuring cups to check. (Conservation of volume — Piaget's classic test. Many 4-year-olds are not yet at conservation stage; this is developmental, not a failure.)
