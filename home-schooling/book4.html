🦁 Unit 1 — Animal Groups

Lesson for Kids

📖 Story Time: The Great Animal Sorting Day

One day, all the animals of the world showed up at the same field — and nobody knew where to stand.

A wise old owl flew up high and called out: "Listen! We are going to sort ourselves into groups. Listen for your group's rules!"

"Group 1: MAMMALS!" called the owl. "You have fur or hair, you breathe air, and your babies drink mother's milk." The lion, the dog, the whale, and the bat all trotted (and swam and flew) to one corner. Even humans went there — yes, we are mammals too!

"Group 2: BIRDS!" The robin, the penguin, and the eagle gathered. "You have feathers, a beak, and you hatch from eggs." (Not all birds fly — but all birds have feathers!)

"Group 3: FISH!" The salmon, the clownfish, and the shark swam to their section. "You live in water, breathe through gills, and have scales."

"Group 4: REPTILES!" The snake, the turtle, the lizard, and the crocodile slid over. "You have scales, you're cold-blooded, and you lay eggs on land."

"Group 5: INSECTS!" The ant, the butterfly, and the beetle marched in. "You have six legs and three body parts." (Spiders have eight legs — they're arachnids, a bonus group!)

The field was sorted. Every animal had a group. Every group had rules. "That," said the owl, "is science — finding the patterns that connect us."

The Five Animal Groups

🦁
Mammals

Fur/hair · warm-blooded · breathe air · nurse young. Examples: dog, whale, bat, human, elephant.

🐦
Birds

Feathers · beak · lay eggs · most fly. Examples: robin, eagle, penguin, parrot, owl.

🐟
Fish

Scales · gills · live in water · cold-blooded. Examples: salmon, shark, clownfish, goldfish.

🦎
Reptiles

Scales · cold-blooded · lay eggs on land. Examples: snake, turtle, lizard, crocodile.

🐜
Insects

Six legs · three body parts (head, thorax, abdomen) · most have wings. Examples: ant, butterfly, bee, beetle.

Activity 1 — Animal Sorting Game

Print or draw pictures of 15 animals (3 from each group). Lay them on the table and ask your child to sort them into five piles. Say the rule for each group as they sort: "This one has feathers and a beak — which group?"

Activity 2 — Habitat Match

Discuss where each animal group lives. Draw four habitats on paper: ocean, forest, desert, sky. Match animal pictures to their habitats. Note that some animals (like ducks — birds that swim!) bridge habitats — this sparks great discussion.

Take turns acting out an animal while others guess. After guessing, ask: "What GROUP is that animal in? How do you know?" This reinforces classification vocabulary through play.

Parent Guide — Unit 1: Animal Groups

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Why classification matters: Sorting and classifying is foundational to both science and mathematics. A child who can group objects by shared properties has mastered a skill used in biology, chemistry, data analysis, and logic throughout their education. Animal groups are the most engaging entry point to this skill.

  1. Emphasize the rules of each group — not just examples. Rules (all mammals have fur; all insects have 6 legs) enable the child to classify NEW animals they've never seen before. This is generalization — the highest level of thinking.
  2. Use edge cases to deepen thinking: "A whale lives in the ocean like a fish — but is it a fish? Why not?" (It breathes air and nurses young — it's a mammal!)
  3. Good books to read alongside: "What Do You Do With a Tail Like This?" by Steve Jenkins, "An Egg Is Quiet" by Dianna Hutts Aston.
  4. If you have a pet, analyze which group it belongs to together. If you see animals on a walk or at the zoo, practice classifying in real time.
✅ Note on amphibians: Frogs and salamanders are a sixth major group — amphibians. They are covered in Unit 2 through the frog life cycle. You can introduce this bonus group when your child is ready.

Assessment — Unit 1: Animal Groups

🦁 Animal Classifier Game (10 minutes)

Q1. Show 10 animal pictures at random. For each: "What group is this in? How do you know?"
Q2. Ask: "Can you name 2 animals that are mammals? 2 birds? 2 insects?"
Q3. Challenge: "A bat flies like a bird — is it a bird?" (It's a mammal — it has fur and nurses young.) Assesses understanding of rules over appearance.
Q4. Ask: "Are humans animals? What group are we in?" — This philosophical question reveals the depth of the child's understanding and always generates wonderful conversation.

🦋 Unit 2 — Life Cycles

Lesson for Kids

📖 Story Time: The Most Amazing Change

Emma found a tiny egg on a leaf — smaller than a pinhead, pale green, and perfectly round.

"What IS that?" she asked her dad.

They watched it for days. One morning, a tiny caterpillar hatched out — bright green with little black stripes. It ate and ate and ATE. Every day it was bigger.

Then one day, the caterpillar went very still and wrapped itself in a silky shell called a chrysalis. Emma checked it every morning. "Is it dead?" she worried.

"No," said her dad. "Something incredible is happening inside."

Two weeks later, the chrysalis cracked open — and out climbed the most beautiful orange-and-black butterfly Emma had ever seen. It stretched its wings slowly, then flew away into the garden.

Emma stood with her mouth open. "The caterpillar BECAME a butterfly?"

"That's called metamorphosis," said her dad. "It's one of the greatest magic tricks in all of nature."

Emma pulled out her notebook. "I'm going to draw every single stage," she announced. "So I never forget."

Activity 1 — Life Cycle Wheel

Draw a circle on a paper plate and divide it into 4 sections. For the butterfly: draw an egg, caterpillar, chrysalis, and butterfly — one in each section. Add arrows between stages. Label each stage (child dictates, you write). Make a separate wheel for the frog.

Activity 2 — Observe the Seed (Continuation from Book 1)

If you planted a seed in Book 1, compare its current state to the early drawings. If starting fresh: plant a bean in a clear-sided cup. Observe and draw the root and shoot emerging over 5–7 days. Ask: "What does the seed need to grow? What would stop it from growing?"

Activity 3 — Life Cycle Sorting Cards

Create or print 8 cards showing mixed stages of the butterfly and frog life cycles. Ask your child to separate them into two groups and then order each group from first to last. This builds sequencing — a key pre-reading and pre-math skill.

💡 Metamorphosis vocabulary: egg, larva/caterpillar, pupa/chrysalis, adult, tadpole, froglet, spawn, metamorphosis, life cycle. Use these real scientific words — children at age 4 love big vocabulary when it's connected to something they find fascinating.

Parent Guide — Unit 2: Life Cycles

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Why life cycles matter: Sequencing (first → next → last) is foundational to reading comprehension, mathematical reasoning, and narrative thinking. Life cycles are the most naturally engaging vehicle for teaching sequence at age 4 because the stages are dramatic, visual, and connected to things children already love — butterflies, frogs, flowers.

  1. Connect life cycles to the child's own growth: "You were a baby — now you're bigger. You're in YOUR life cycle too!" This makes the concept personal and emotionally resonant.
  2. If at all possible, observe a real life cycle: raise a caterpillar (many kits available online), watch tadpoles in a pond, or grow a sunflower from seed to flower.
  3. Ask predictive questions: "What do you think the caterpillar is doing inside the chrysalis? What do you think a tadpole needs that a frog doesn't?"
  4. Distinguish metamorphosis (dramatic body-change: butterfly, frog) from growth without metamorphosis (humans, dogs) — this is an excellent early biology distinction.
❤️ Recommended read-aloud: "The Very Hungry Caterpillar" by Eric Carle (also reinforces days of the week, numbers 1–5, and food vocabulary!). "Jump, Frog, Jump!" by Robert Kalan for the frog cycle.

Assessment — Unit 2: Life Cycles

🦋 Life Cycle Sequence Check (10 minutes)

Q1. Lay out 4 butterfly stage cards out of order. Ask: "Can you put these in the right order — from the very beginning to the adult butterfly?"
Q2. Ask: "What comes after the egg in the butterfly life cycle? What comes after the tadpole?"
Q3. Ask: "What is inside the chrysalis? What is metamorphosis?" — Tests vocabulary retention and conceptual understanding.
Q4. Ask: "Does a plant have a life cycle? What is the very first stage?" Assesses transfer of the concept from animals to plants.

⚙️ Unit 3 — Simple Machines

Lesson for Kids

📖 Story Time: The Problem Solvers

Sam had a very heavy box of books to move to the top shelf. She tried to lift it straight up — too heavy!

Her brother said: "Use the ramp! Slide it up the inclined plane instead of lifting straight up." Sam pushed the box up the ramp — much easier! "The ramp is a simple machine!" her brother explained. "It doesn't do the work FOR you — it makes the work easier."

Outside, their little sister was on a see-saw. "That's a LEVER," said Sam. "A lever is a bar that rests on a point — the fulcrum. One end goes down, the other goes up."

Dad was fixing his bike. "The wheel on my bike is a WHEEL AND AXLE," he said. "It lets you travel far with little effort."

Mom used scissors to cut paper. "Scissors are two WEDGES joined together," she said. "A sharp wedge splits things apart."

"Simple machines are EVERYWHERE!" Sam realized. "Stairs are a ramp. A doorknob is a wheel. A nail is a wedge. Our whole world is built from six simple machines!"

The Six Simple Machines

MachineWhat It DoesExamples at Home
⚖️ LeverBar on a fulcrum — lifts or moves loadsSee-saw, crowbar, bottle opener, scissors handle
🔵 Wheel & AxleWheel on a rod — reduces friction, moves thingsBicycle, door knob, rolling pin, toy car
📐 Inclined Plane (Ramp)Slanted surface — reduces force needed to liftRamp, staircase, slide, loading ramp
🔩 ScrewInclined plane wrapped in a spiral — holds or drillsJar lid, wood screw, spiral staircase, bolt
🪓 WedgeTwo inclined planes — splits things apartKnife, nail, axe, zipper teeth, doorstop
🪢 PulleyWheel + rope — changes direction or multiplies forceFlagpole, window blind, crane, well bucket

Activity 1 — Ramp Experiment

Set up a ramp with a plank of wood propped on books. Roll a toy car down. Try different heights — steeper ramp vs. shallower ramp. Ask: "Which ramp makes the car go farther? Why?" This is a full mini-experiment: predict, test, observe, conclude.

Activity 2 — Lever Play

Balance a ruler on a pencil (the fulcrum). Place a block on one end and push down on the other. Move the fulcrum closer to the load — less effort needed! Ask: "Why is a see-saw easier than just lifting your friend?"

Activity 3 — Simple Machine Hunt

Walk through your home and find one example of each of the six simple machines. Draw each one and label what type of machine it is. Some objects combine two machines (scissors = lever + wedge!) — double points for spotting those!

✅ The big idea: Simple machines don't create energy — they redirect or multiply the force we apply, making work easier. This concept seeds every future lesson in physics.

Parent Guide — Unit 3: Simple Machines

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Why engineering thinking at age 4: Engineering is the application of science to solve real problems. Introducing simple machines early gives children a framework for seeing the physical world as designed — not just observed. This "designer's eye" supports STEM pathways and creative problem-solving throughout life.

  1. Focus on only 3 machines for deep understanding: ramp (inclined plane), lever, and wheel. Introduce the other three as bonus discoveries.
  2. Always connect to real objects the child uses: "When you ride your bike, the wheels are doing simple machine work right now!"
  3. Use the scientific method language: "What do you PREDICT will happen? Let's TEST it. What did you OBSERVE? What does that tell us?" — 4-year-olds can handle this framework with guidance.
  4. Build something: even a simple tower of blocks knocked over by a ramp-launched toy car is engineering in action.
💡 Vocabulary bridge: Force (push or pull), effort (the force you apply), load (the thing you're moving), fulcrum (the resting point of a lever). Using correct vocabulary from the start is an academic gift.

Assessment — Unit 3: Simple Machines

⚙️ Machine Spotter (10 minutes)

Q1. Show pictures of a ramp, lever, and wheel. Ask: "What type of machine is this? What does it do?"
Q2. Ask: "Why is a ramp easier than lifting something straight up?" — Tests conceptual understanding, not just naming.
Q3. Hold up a pair of scissors. Ask: "Is this a simple machine? What kind?" (Lever + wedge — bonus if they say both!)
Q4. Ask: "Can you think of a problem at home that a simple machine could help solve?" — Creative engineering thinking.

📝 Unit 4 — First Sentences

Lesson for Kids

📖 Story Time: Three Words That Say Everything

Lily could read lots of words now. But words alone felt like puzzle pieces — amazing, but not the whole picture.

Her teacher said: "Today we put words together into sentences. A sentence has two parts: someone (who we're talking about) and something they DO."

She held up three cards: The · cat · sat.

"The cat sat!" Lily read. She looked at the picture — a striped cat, perfectly sitting. "I read a SENTENCE!"

"The dog ran. The bug is red. I can hop." Each one was three words. Each one was a whole idea — a tiny story!

"Every book ever written," said her teacher, "is made of sentences just like these. The fanciest novel in the library started with someone who knew how to write three words together."

Lily picked up her pencil. She wrote, very carefully: I am big.

It was her first sentence. It was perfect.

How a Simple Sentence Works

Every sentence has a subject (who or what) and a predicate (what they do or are). At this stage, we build 3-word sentences:

The cat
sat.
The dog
ran.
I
can
hop.
The bug
is

Activity 1 — Sentence Card Flip

Make two sets of cards: Subject cards (the cat, the dog, I, a frog) and Predicate cards (sat, ran, can hop, is big). Flip one from each pile and read the combined sentence. Some combinations will be silly — celebrate the silliness! "The frog is big!" and "The dog sat" are both perfectly valid.

Activity 2 — Read and Draw

Write a simple sentence on paper. Ask your child to read it, then draw a picture of what it says. Start with: "The cat is big." "A red bug sat." "The dog can run." Drawing the meaning proves comprehension — reading is not just decoding sounds, it's understanding meaning.

Activity 3 — Write My First Sentence

Ask your child: "Tell me something true about yourself." Help them shape it into a sentence. Write it in large dotted letters for them to trace, then copy. Example: "I am four." "I can run." "I like dogs." Display this first written sentence on the fridge — it is a landmark.

✅ Sentence rules to introduce gently: Sentences start with a capital letter. Sentences end with a period (or ! or ?). These become habits through repeated exposure, not memorized rules.

Parent Guide — Unit 4: First Sentences

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

The leap from words to sentences: Decoding individual words is phonics. Reading sentences is the first step toward reading comprehension — understanding meaning, not just sounds. This is a qualitative leap in literacy and deserves celebration. The goal this unit: fluent reading of 3-word sentences with full comprehension.

  1. Point to each word as you read together — this reinforces left-to-right directionality, word boundaries, and print tracking.
  2. Introduce a few high-frequency "sight words" that don't follow phonics rules but appear in almost every sentence: the, is, I, a, can, am, it. These are worth memorizing as whole shapes.
  3. For writing sentences: have the child dictate while you write first, then let them copy. Move to independent writing only after they're confident with the words.
  4. Read simple readers together — the "Bob Books" series or "Elephant and Piggie" by Mo Willems are perfect for this stage.
⚠️ Spelling at this stage: Accept invented spelling ("I cn hop" for "I can hop") without correction — it means your child is applying phonics rules independently. Celebrate the thinking, gently show the standard spelling alongside: "Yes! And grown-ups write 'can' like this."

Assessment — Unit 4: First Sentences

📝 Sentence Reader Check (10 minutes)

Q1. Show 5 simple sentences (no pictures). Ask the child to read each one aloud. Note: Are they blending all words? Are they reading the sentence as a whole idea?
Q2. Read a sentence aloud and ask: "Draw what that sentence means." Check that the drawing matches the sentence — tests comprehension.
Q3. Ask the child to write one sentence about what they did today. Accept any attempt — observe pencil grip, left-to-right direction, capital letter, period.
Q4. Show a sentence with a deliberate error (no capital letter or no period). Ask: "Is there anything missing from this sentence?"

➖ Unit 5 — Subtraction to 10

Lesson for Kids

📖 Story Time: The Disappearing Cookies

There were 8 cookies on the plate. The dog snuck in and ate 3.

"How many cookies are LEFT?" asked Mom.

Zara counted what was still on the plate: "1, 2, 3, 4, 5. Five cookies!"

"You just did subtraction!" said Mom. "We started with 8. We TOOK AWAY 3. We have 5 left. 8 minus 3 equals 5."

Zara looked at the dog. "The dog knows subtraction."

Mom laughed. "Subtraction is just addition backwards. When we add, things come together. When we subtract, things go away. They're partners — you can't have one without the other."

Zara put 5 blocks on the table. She took away 2. "5 take away 2 is 3!" She put 7 pennies in a row. She covered 4 with her hand. "7 take away 4 is 3!"

"I think I could do this all day," she said.

"Save some cookies for the rest of us," said Mom.

Activity 1 — Take-Away Stories

Use 10 objects (blocks, grapes, toy animals). Tell a take-away story: "There were 7 frogs on a log. 3 jumped in the pond. How many are still on the log?" Have the child physically remove the objects and count what remains.

Story ProblemObjects to UseNumber Sentence
8 birds on a branch. 3 fly away. How many stay?8 blocks, remove 38 − 3 = 5
6 fish in a bowl. 2 swim out. How many left?6 crackers, eat 26 − 2 = 4
10 balloons. 4 pop. How many remain?10 fingers, fold 4 down10 − 4 = 6

Activity 2 — Addition & Subtraction Partners

Show that addition and subtraction undo each other: "Put 3 blocks with 4 blocks — how many? 7! Now take away 4 — how many? 3 again!" This lays the foundation for "fact families" — the understanding that 3 + 4 = 7, 4 + 3 = 7, 7 − 3 = 4, and 7 − 4 = 3 are all related.

Activity 3 — Finger Subtraction

Hold up 8 fingers. Fold down 5. How many are still up? Fingers are the most reliable, always-available manipulative. Make finger math a natural habit for solving any problem under 10.

💡 Key language: Use all of these terms interchangeably — take away, minus, subtract, how many are left, what remains, how many fewer. Rich vocabulary now prevents confusion with formal math notation later.

Parent Guide — Unit 5: Subtraction

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Subtraction as the inverse of addition: The most powerful insight in early arithmetic is that addition and subtraction are partners — knowing one helps you know the other. If a child knows 5 + 3 = 8, they can work out 8 − 5 = ? by thinking addition. This "inverse operation" insight, built early, dramatically accelerates math fact fluency.

  1. Always start with physical objects — never abstract symbols alone. The concrete experience must precede the notation.
  2. Introduce subtraction as "taking away" first. Later, introduce it as "finding the difference" (how many more?). Both are subtraction but feel different to children.
  3. Create fact family trios on index cards: 3 / 4 / 7 → show how 3+4, 4+3, 7−3, 7−4 all belong to the same number family.
  4. Celebrate the "zero" result: "If I have 5 apples and eat 5, I have ZERO left. Zero is a real number — it just means none."
✅ Subtraction facts to focus on: Any subtraction with a result ≤ 5 first (5−2, 4−1, 3−3). Then extend to differences ≤ 10. Mastery is not the goal at age 4 — familiarity is.

Assessment — Unit 5: Subtraction

➖ Take-Away Check (10 minutes)

Q1. Place 9 objects. Ask the child to take away 4. Ask: "How many are left?" Observe counting strategy.
Q2. Tell a story problem: "You have 6 stickers. You give 2 to your friend. How many do you have now?"
Q3. Show an addition fact (3 + 5 = 8). Ask: "If we know that, what is 8 take away 5?" — Tests inverse operation understanding (advanced — celebrate any attempt).
Q4. Ask: "What is 4 take away 4?" — Tests zero concept. Then: "What is 7 take away 0?" — Subtraction by zero is a conceptual challenge worth exploring.

🕐 Unit 6 — Telling Time

Lesson for Kids

📖 Story Time: The Clock That Never Stops

Eli noticed the clock on the wall had two hands — a short one and a long one. They were always moving. Tick. Tick. Tick.

"What are the two hands for?" he asked his grandpa.

"The SHORT hand," said Grandpa, "points to the HOUR. It's the big number — it tells you which hour of the day it is."

"And the LONG hand?"

"The long hand counts the MINUTES. When the long hand points straight UP — to the 12 — it's exactly on the hour. Like right now — look!"

Eli looked. Both hands: the short one pointed at the 8, the long one pointed up at the 12. "8 o'clock!" he said.

"Exactly right. And when the long hand points straight DOWN — to the 6 — that means it's half past the hour. Halfway through."

The clock ticked on. Eli watched it, fascinated. Time was moving all the time — you just had to know how to read it.

"Time is one of the most precious things there is," said Grandpa. "Learning to tell it is how you take charge of your day."

Reading the Clock

🕐
O'clock (e.g., 3:00)

Short hand → points at the hour number (3)
Long hand → points straight UP at 12
We say: "3 o'clock"

🕞
Half past (e.g., 3:30)

Short hand → between 3 and 4
Long hand → points straight DOWN at 6
We say: "half past 3" or "3:30"

Activity 1 — Paper Plate Clock

Make a clock from a paper plate: write numbers 1–12 around the edge, cut a short and long hand from cardboard, attach with a brad. Practice setting the clock to o'clock times as you call them out. Then try half-past times.

Activity 2 — Daily Schedule Clock

Make a simple daily schedule with 4–5 key times: wake up (7:00), breakfast (7:30), lunch (12:00), nap (1:00), dinner (6:00). For each, set the paper plate clock together. Say the time. Ask: "What do we do at this time?"

Activity 3 — Time Hunt

Find every clock in your home (microwave, stove, wall clock, alarm clock, wristwatch). Read each one together. Ask: "Are they all showing the same time? Why might they be different?"

⚠️ Analog first, digital second. Teaching time on an analog clock builds genuine number sense (the clock face is a number line wrapped in a circle). Digital clocks are easy to read but teach nothing about how time works. Master analog first.

Parent Guide — Unit 6: Telling Time

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Why telling time matters at age 4: Time-telling builds number sense, sequential reasoning, and self-regulation (a child who can read a clock can understand "5 more minutes" concretely). The Common Core expects children to tell time to the hour and half-hour by end of first grade — building the foundation now makes that milestone natural.

  1. Start with only o'clock times for 1–2 weeks. Add half-past only when o'clock is confident.
  2. Refer to the actual clock throughout the day: "It's 7 o'clock — time for breakfast! What does 7 o'clock look like on our clock?"
  3. Emphasize the SHORT hand as "the boss" — it tells the hour, which is the most important piece of information. The long hand refines it.
  4. Use the paper-plate clock daily as a "clock quiz" — set it and ask them to read it, or say a time and ask them to set it.
💡 Math connection: The clock face introduces the concept of a circle divided into equal parts — a foundation for fractions (half, quarter) and eventually degrees in geometry. When a child masters "half past," they have internalized the concept of one-half in a spatial context.

Assessment — Unit 6: Telling Time

🕐 Clock Reader Check (10 minutes)

Q1. Set the paper-plate clock to 5 o'clock. Ask: "What time is it?" Repeat for 2 o'clock, 9 o'clock, 11 o'clock.
Q2. Set the clock to half past 3. Ask: "What time is it?" Repeat for half past 7 and half past 10.
Q3. Say a time (e.g., "4 o'clock") and ask the child to set the paper-plate clock to show it. Tests productive understanding vs. just receptive recognition.
Q4. Ask: "What time do you wake up? Can you show me on the clock?" — Connects abstract time-telling to personal daily life.

Storytelling Activity — Make Your Own Tale

  1. Choose a character: a brave mouse, a flying elephant, a robot who can't stop dancing.
  2. Give them a problem: they are lost, they broke something, they want something they can't reach.
  3. Tell the story out loud — Beginning, Middle, End.
  4. Draw a 3-panel comic strip: one box per story part.
  5. Write (or dictate) one sentence under each box.
❤️ The ultimate goal: A child who can construct and tell a story with a beginning, middle, and end has mastered narrative structure — the same structure used in every essay, report, presentation, and book they will ever write. This skill, built at age 4 through playful storytelling, compounds for a lifetime.
