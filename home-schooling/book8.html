🔤 Unit 1 — Advanced Phonics

Lesson for Kids

📖 The Secret Letter Teams

Some letters like to work in pairs. When they work together, they make a totally new sound — not the sound of either letter alone.

SH: S and H together say /sh/ — like "shhhh, be quiet!" (ship, shell, fish, brush)

CH: C and H say /ch/ — like a little train: ch-ch-ch! (chip, lunch, rich, chair)

TH: T and H say /th/ — two versions: voiced (the, this, that) and unvoiced (think, bath, teeth)

The Magic E rule: When a word ends in E, the E is silent but it makes the vowel in the middle say its name: c-a-k-e → the A says "ay" not "ah". cape, bike, hope, cube, kite — the silent E is magic!

Activity — Word Sort

Write 12 words on cards — mix of CVC and CVCE (cap/cape, bit/bite, hop/hope, etc.). Ask the child to sort them into two piles: "short vowel" and "long vowel / Magic E." Read each one aloud. The contrast between pairs (cap vs. cape) makes the Magic E rule immediately clear.

Parent Guide — Unit 1: Advanced Phonics

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Why digraphs and CVCE matter: These two phonics patterns appear in the vast majority of early reading texts. A child who has mastered CVC words (Book 3) and can now apply digraphs and Magic E can decode 80%+ of words in early reader books. This is the gateway to independent reading for pleasure.

  1. Digraphs first: SH and CH are the most common and easiest. TH has two versions — teach the voiced version first (the, this) since it's more frequent.
  2. The Magic E rule is powerful and children find it delightful — the "silent E makes the vowel say its name" framing is memorable and accurate.
  3. Blends are NOT digraphs — in a blend (bl, cr, st) you can still hear both letter sounds, just quickly. Help the child hear the difference.
  4. Find these patterns in the books you're reading together — connecting phonics instruction to real reading texts is the most effective practice.

Assessment — Unit 1

🔤 Phonics Check (10 min)

Q1. Show words: ship, chat, that, when. Ask the child to read each and identify the digraph.
Q2. Show: flag, crab, stop, tree. Ask the child to read each and identify the blend.
Q3. Show word pairs: kit/kite, cap/cape. Ask: "What changed? Why does the vowel sound change?"
Q4. Give 5 unfamiliar CVCE words (e.g., "dune," "robe," "pine"). Can the child decode them independently?

📚 Unit 2 — Vocabulary: Word Power

Lesson for Kids

📖 The Word Collector

Zara collected words the way some kids collect trading cards. When she heard a new word — a beautiful word, a surprising word, a word that sounded exactly like what it meant — she wrote it in her Word Notebook.

"Synonyms," her teacher said, "are words that mean almost the same thing. Happy, joyful, elated, content — they're all in the happy family, but each has its own flavor."

"Antonyms are opposites: hot and cold, happy and sad, gigantic and tiny."

"And compound words are two words that join together to make a new meaning: sun + flower = sunflower. Rain + bow = rainbow. Butter + fly = butterfly."

Zara opened her notebook. "I want 1,000 words," she announced. "By the end of the year."

Her teacher smiled. "Start today."

Vocabulary Building Blocks

Synonyms

Words with similar meanings. happy → glad, joyful, cheerful. big → large, huge, enormous.

Antonyms

Words with opposite meanings. hot ↔ cold. fast ↔ slow. light ↔ dark. happy ↔ sad.

Compound Words

Two words joined: sunflower, rainbow, butterfly, bedroom, backyard, footprint.

Adjectives

Words that describe nouns: the fluffy cat, the tiny ant, the enormous elephant, the sparkling star.

Activity 1 — The Synonym Ladder

Start with a common adjective: "big." Draw a ladder. Each rung is a synonym that is slightly more intense: big → large → huge → enormous → gigantic → colossal. This teaches shades of meaning — not just vocabulary, but precision of expression.

Activity 2 — Compound Word Snap

Write 20 words on cards (10 pairs that can combine): sun+flower, rain+bow, butter+fly, cup+cake, bed+room, tooth+brush, door+bell, rain+coat, play+ground, back+pack. Match pairs, say the compound word, use it in a sentence.

✅ Word Notebook: Give your child a small notebook dedicated to new words. Any time they hear or read a word they don't know — or a word that is delightfully expressive — they write it in their notebook. Revisit it weekly. This single habit, started at age 4, produces extraordinary vocabulary by age 10.

Parent Guide — Unit 2: Vocabulary

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Vocabulary is the #1 predictor of reading comprehension: Research by E.D. Hirsch Jr. and others consistently shows that vocabulary knowledge — not decoding ability — is the strongest predictor of reading comprehension from 3rd grade onward. Every word a child learns before formal schooling gives them a cognitive advantage that compounds over decades.

  1. Never talk down to a child's vocabulary. Use the most precise word available: not "a lot" but "enormous"; not "said" but "whispered" or "announced."
  2. When the child asks what a word means, define it with examples and context — not just dictionary definitions. "Transparent means you can see right through it — like a window, or a glass of water."
  3. Read-alouds expose children to vocabulary 2–3 grade levels above their reading level. Never stop reading aloud.
  4. Play word games: "Can you think of a word that means the opposite of angry?" "Can you think of three words that describe this sunset?"

Assessment — Unit 2

📚 Word Power Check (10 min)

Q1. "Give me 2 synonyms for 'happy.' For 'big.' For 'walk.'"
Q2. "What is the antonym of 'hot'? 'Noisy'? 'Ancient'?"
Q3. Show compound word halves. Ask the child to pair them and say the compound word.
Q4. Show a sentence: "The cat sat on the mat." Ask: "Can you make this sentence more interesting by adding adjectives?" (e.g., "The fluffy orange cat sat on the soft purple mat.")

📰 Unit 3 — Non-Fiction Reading

Lesson for Kids

📖 Reading to Learn vs. Reading for Fun

There are two kinds of books in the library. Fiction books tell made-up stories — the characters and events are invented by the author's imagination. Non-fiction books tell true information — facts about animals, history, science, and how the world works.

Non-fiction looks different from fiction. It has features that help you find information: a table of contents that lists what's inside, headings in big letters that name each section, captions under pictures that explain what you're looking at, and an index in the back to find specific topics.

Non-fiction also has opinions mixed with facts — and a smart reader learns to tell the difference. A fact can be checked and proven true: "Dogs have four legs." An opinion is what someone thinks or feels: "Dogs are the best pets." Both sentences can be true — but only one is a fact.

Text Features to Recognize

FeatureWhat It DoesWhere to Find It
TitleNames the book/article — tells the main topicCover and first page
Table of ContentsLists chapters/sections with page numbersFront of the book
Heading/SubheadingNames each section — tells what that part is aboutBeginning of each section
CaptionExplains what a photo or diagram showsUnder or beside pictures
Bold wordsImportant vocabulary words highlighted for attentionThroughout the text
IndexAlphabetical list of topics with page numbersBack of the book

Activity — Fact or Opinion Sort

Write 10 statements on cards, 5 facts and 5 opinions (about animals, food, or places your child knows). Have the child sort them into two piles. Discuss how you know which is which: "Can we look this up and check? Then it's a fact. Is it what someone thinks or prefers? Then it's an opinion."

Activity — Non-Fiction Feature Hunt

Find a children's non-fiction book or nature magazine. Go on a text feature scavenger hunt: find the table of contents, a heading, a caption, a bold word. Discuss what each one helps the reader do.

Parent Guide — Unit 3: Non-Fiction

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Why non-fiction literacy matters: By 4th grade, the majority of reading assigned in school is non-fiction. Children who have only read stories struggle enormously with textbooks, articles, and informational texts. Building non-fiction reading habits before formal school is one of the best academic investments possible.

  1. Read non-fiction books alongside fiction — one of each per week is ideal. National Geographic Kids, DK Eyewitness, and Let's-Read-and-Find-Out Science books are excellent.
  2. Practice the fact/opinion distinction in daily conversation: "Is that a fact or your opinion? How could we find out for sure?"
  3. Teach the child to use the table of contents and index — navigating information, not just absorbing it, is a critical skill.
  4. Connect to writing: when the child writes a paragraph in Unit 4, ask them to include at least one fact they can verify.

Assessment — Unit 3

📰 Non-Fiction Reader Check (10 min)

Q1. Open a non-fiction book. Ask: "What text features can you find? What does each one tell you?"
Q2. Read 2–3 sentences from a non-fiction book aloud. Ask: "What did you learn? Is that a fact or an opinion?"
Q3. "What is the difference between a fiction book and a non-fiction book? Can you give an example of each?"
Q4. "If I wanted to find information about penguins in a non-fiction book, where would I look first?" (Table of contents or index.)

🗓️ Unit 4 — Personal Narratives

Lesson for Kids

📖 Your Story Is Worth Telling

A personal narrative is a true story about something that happened to YOU. You are the main character. You were really there. And because it happened to you, no one in the world can tell it better.

Good personal narratives have three things: what happened (in order), what you felt (emotions and reactions), and what you noticed (sensory details — what you saw, heard, smelled, touched, tasted).

Don't write: "We went to the beach." Write: "The cold waves crashed over my feet and I screamed with surprise and then laughed so hard I fell down in the sand, which got in my mouth and tasted like salt and sunscreen."

See the difference? Details bring writing to life.

The Personal Narrative Structure

PartPurposeExample Opening
HookStart with the most interesting moment"I was hanging upside-down from the monkey bars when it happened."
BackgroundSet the scene — when, where, who"It was the first day of summer at Grandma's house."
EventsWhat happened, in order"First… then… next… finally…"
FeelingsHow you felt and why"I felt proud because I had never done that before."
EndingWhat you learned or realized"That day I learned that being scared doesn't mean you have to stop."

Activity — The Small Moment Story

The best personal narratives are about small, specific moments — not big events. Not "my whole summer" but "the five minutes I found a caterpillar." Ask your child: "Think of something interesting that happened to you — even something tiny." That's their story. Guide them through the five-part structure above.

Parent Guide — Unit 4: Personal Narratives

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Why personal narratives are foundational: Personal narrative is the first non-fiction writing genre taught in every US elementary curriculum. It is also the most motivating — because the child is the expert on their own life. The habits built here (sensory details, chronological structure, emotional honesty) transfer to every other form of writing.

  1. The "small moment" strategy is from Lucy Calkins' Writing Workshop — the most widely used writing curriculum in US schools. A small, specific moment is always more vivid and powerful than a broad summary.
  2. Ask sensory questions to expand the writing: "What did you see? What did you hear? What did the air feel like? What did you want to do?"
  3. Share your own personal narratives with your child — model the genre before asking them to write in it.
  4. Read excellent mentor texts for personal narrative: "The Relatives Came" by Cynthia Rylant, "Shortcut" by Donald Crews, "When I Was Young in the Mountains" by Cynthia Rylant.

Assessment — Unit 4

🗓️ Narrative Check (10 min)

Q1. Read the child's personal narrative aloud. Ask: "Can you find the hook? The feelings? The ending?"
Q2. Ask: "Is there a place where you could add more detail? What did it smell like? What did you hear?"
Q3. Ask: "Why did you choose this moment to write about? What made it special?" — Tests metacognitive awareness.
Q4. Ask the child to read their narrative aloud to a family member — this is the real audience test. Watch how the listener responds and share that reaction with the child as feedback.

🧚 Unit 5 — Fiction Writing: Inventing Worlds

Lesson for Kids

📖 You Are the Author

When you write fiction, you are a GOD of a tiny world. You decide who lives in it, what the place looks like, what problems arise, and how they are solved. You have total power — and total responsibility to make it interesting!

Every fiction story needs four things:

Characters: Who is in the story? What do they want? What are they afraid of?

Setting: Where and when does the story happen? A rainy city? A magical forest? The surface of the moon in 2250?

Problem: What goes wrong? The best problems make the character uncomfortable, challenged, or forced to grow.

Solution: How does the character solve (or fail to solve) the problem? What do they learn?

Story Planning Map

Story ElementQuestions to AnswerExample
🧑 CharacterName? Age? Special quality? Weakness?Mira, age 7, can talk to animals but is terrified of water
🏞️ SettingWhere? When? What does it look, smell, sound like?A floating island city above the clouds, where it rains upward
😟 ProblemWhat goes wrong? Why is this a challenge for THIS character?A baby dragon falls from the island and Mira must dive into clouds to save it
💡 SolutionHow is it solved? Does the character grow?Mira faces her fear of falling and discovers she can fly when she believes in herself

Activity — Mini Fiction Book

Fold 4 sheets of paper in half and staple: a 16-page book. Title page, one page per scene (setting, problem, turning point, solution), illustrations throughout. The child dictates the text; you write it or they write it themselves. This is their first published book.

Parent Guide — Unit 5: Fiction Writing

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Why fiction writing builds intelligence: Creating characters requires theory of mind (imagining how others think and feel). Creating a coherent plot requires causal reasoning (this leads to that). Creating a setting requires descriptive language and spatial imagination. Together, fiction writing exercises more cognitive systems simultaneously than almost any other early childhood activity.

  1. Let the child's imagination lead completely — resist the urge to make the story more "sensible." A dragon who is also a chef who is also a time traveler is perfect.
  2. Ask the author's questions: "Why would your character do that? What are they feeling right now? What happens next?"
  3. Use the "yes, and" principle from improv theatre: always build on what the child offers, never redirect away from their idea.
  4. After the story is written, read it aloud together and celebrate it as a real piece of literature. Bind it, display it, share it — treat it with the respect any author's work deserves.

Assessment — Unit 5

🧚 Fiction Writing Check (10 min)

Q1. Ask: "Who is your main character? What do they want? What are they afraid of?" Depth of character = depth of story.
Q2. Ask: "What is the problem in your story? Why is it hard for your character to solve?"
Q3. Ask: "Read me your favorite sentence from your story." — Self-evaluation of quality.
Q4. Ask: "If you could write a sequel — what would happen next?" Open-ended creativity.

🎤 Unit 6 — Oral Presentation & Poetry

Lesson for Kids

📖 Your Voice Is a Gift

You have been learning to put words on paper. But words spoken aloud — with the right tone, the right speed, the right expression — can move people in ways no written word can alone. When you stand up to speak, you are using the oldest form of human communication. Stories were spoken before they were written. Songs were sung before they were notated.

Poetry lives in the music of language: rhyme (words that end with the same sound), rhythm (the beat of the words), and imagery (pictures painted with words — "The moon is a silver coin" is an image, not a fact).

Your voice is a gift. Use it clearly, proudly, and expressively — and the whole room will listen.

A Poem to Learn by Heart

The Road Not Taken (simplified for children) — after Robert Frost

Two roads went off into the wood,
I could not take them both, although I would.
I took the one less traveled by,
And that has made all the difference.

Write Your Own Poem — The "I Am" Poem

LineTemplateExample
1I am (two special things about you)I am curious and kind.
2I wonder (something you're curious about)I wonder if fish dream.
3I hear (an imaginary sound)I hear the stars humming.
4I see (an imaginary sight)I see colors no one has named yet.
5I want (a deep wish)I want to understand every language.
6I am (repeat line 1)I am curious and kind.

Activity — Author's Chair Presentation

Set up a special chair. The child sits in it as the "author." They read their personal narrative OR their fiction story OR their poem to a real audience (family, grandparent, neighbor on video call). Practice: clear voice, good pace, look up occasionally. This is public speaking — the skill consistently rated #1 most valuable by adults in every profession.

❤️ The audience matters: A child who has a real audience for their work — not just their parent, but a grandparent, an aunt, a family friend — develops authentic author pride that no classroom certificate can replicate. Create the audience. It changes everything.

Parent Guide — Unit 6: Oral & Poetry

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Oral language and literacy are inseparable: Children with strong oral language skills consistently become stronger readers and writers. A child who speaks in full, expressive sentences has already internalized the grammar, vocabulary, and narrative structures needed for excellent literacy.

  1. Record the presentation on video — watching themselves speak is enormously informative and motivating for children.
  2. Poetry memorization is one of the oldest and most powerful educational traditions. "The Road Not Taken" simplified version, the Pledge of Allegiance, nursery rhymes — all of these build phonological awareness, vocabulary, and cultural literacy.
  3. The "I Am" poem template produces deeply personal, beautiful writing every time. Frame and display the finished poem.
  4. Teach presentation skills gently: speak slowly, make eye contact, use your hands. These are life skills that will serve the child in every presentation, job interview, and relationship of their life.

Assessment — Unit 6

🎤 Oral & Poetry Check

Q1. Ask the child to recite the memorized poem from memory. Celebrate any attempt — the act of memorizing is the achievement.
Q2. Ask: "What is a rhyme? Can you give me 3 words that rhyme with 'night'?"
Q3. Watch back the Author's Chair video (if recorded). Ask: "What did you do well? What would you change?" — Self-reflection builds metacognition.
Q4. Ask: "What is one image in your 'I Am' poem? What does it make you picture?"
