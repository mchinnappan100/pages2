🏛️ Unit 1 — American History: Our Nation's Story

Lesson for Kids

📖 Story Time: The Long Story of America

America's story is very, very long — and it begins long before the United States existed.

For thousands of years, Indigenous peoples — Native Americans — lived across the land. They built cities, farmed corn and beans, told stories, and developed hundreds of different languages and cultures. The Cherokee, Lakota, Iroquois, and hundreds of other nations called this land home.

Then, in 1492, Christopher Columbus sailed from Spain and arrived in the Caribbean. More European explorers followed. In 1620, a group of English settlers called the Pilgrims crossed the ocean on a ship called the Mayflower. They were looking for a new home where they could practice their religion freely. The Wampanoag people helped them survive their first winter.

Over time, more and more colonists came. But they were still ruled by the King of England far away — and he made rules they thought were unfair. So in 1776, the colonists declared: "We are FREE. We are a new nation!" They wrote the Declaration of Independence, and after a long war, the United States of America was born.

"This country is made of people from everywhere," said the teacher. "Every person who has come here has added something to our story."

American History Timeline

Thousands of Years Ago — Indigenous Peoples

Native American nations build rich civilizations across North America — farming, trading, storytelling, governing.

1492 — Columbus Arrives

European exploration of the Americas begins. Many difficult changes follow for Indigenous peoples.

1620 — The Pilgrims & the Mayflower

102 passengers cross the Atlantic. The Wampanoag people teach them to grow food. They share a harvest feast — the origin of Thanksgiving.

1776 — Declaration of Independence

The 13 colonies declare independence from Britain. Thomas Jefferson writes: "All men are created equal."

1789 — First President

George Washington becomes the first President of the United States. The Constitution is ratified.

Activity — My American Heritage Interview

Ask a grandparent or older family member: "Where did our family come from? When did they come to America? What did they bring with them?" Draw a picture of one thing they shared. Every family's story is part of America's story.

Parent Guide — Unit 1: US History

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Teaching history truthfully at age 4–5: Present American history honestly and age-appropriately — acknowledging both achievements and difficult truths. When discussing Columbus or colonial history, you can say: "The arrival of Europeans was very hard for the people who already lived here. Many were treated unjustly. Knowing this helps us be better." Children this age can handle honest, non-traumatic truths.

  1. Use picture books: "When We Were Alone" by David A. Robertson, "Encounter" by Jane Yolen, "The Story of America" series by DK.
  2. Connect to American holidays your family observes — Fourth of July, Thanksgiving, Presidents' Day — as living history.
  3. Visit a local historical site, museum, or national park if possible — first-hand history is unforgettable.
  4. Emphasize the ongoing, unfinished nature of the American story: "Our country is still working to make sure everyone is truly equal. That's a job for all of us — including you."

Assessment — Unit 1

🏛️ History Check (10 min)

Q1. "Who lived in America before the Europeans arrived?"
Q2. "What was the Mayflower? Who sailed on it?"
Q3. "What does the Declaration of Independence say?"
Q4. "What are the 50 stars on our flag for?"

🌐 Unit 2 — World Cultures

Lesson for Kids

📖 Story Time: A Table with Many Dishes

Imagine a big table where every culture in the world has brought a dish to share. There would be fragrant Indian curry and sticky Japanese sushi. There'd be Nigerian jollof rice and Mexican tamales and Italian pasta and American apple pie. The table would never end — because there are thousands of cultures in the world, and each one has brought something beautiful.

Every culture has its own music, clothing, stories, celebrations, and ways of greeting each other. Some bow when they say hello. Some shake hands. Some press noses. Some hug. There are as many ways to say "I see you and I respect you" as there are people on Earth.

"Different doesn't mean wrong," said Grandma. "It means the world is larger and more interesting than any one person can imagine on their own."

Five Cultures to Explore

🎎
Japan

Origami, cherry blossoms, bowing as greeting, sushi, koi fish, Mount Fuji.

🌮
Mexico

Día de los Muertos, mariachi music, tamales, colorful textiles, ancient Maya pyramids.

🥁
Nigeria

Vibrant drumming and dance, jollof rice, Yoruba and Igbo languages, rich storytelling tradition.

🪘
India

Diwali (festival of lights), classical dance, curry, the Taj Mahal, yoga.

🏔️
Peru

Machu Picchu, Quechua language, quinoa, colorful weavings, Andes mountains.

Activity 1 — Culture Research Poster

Choose one culture from the grid (or any culture your family connects to). Draw or cut out pictures representing: one food, one celebration, one traditional clothing item, one famous landmark or natural feature. Make a poster titled "I Am Learning About [Culture Name]."

Activity 2 — Cook a World Recipe Together

Choose one simple recipe from a different culture: Japanese onigiri (rice balls), Mexican guacamole, Indian mango lassi, or Nigerian bean cakes. Cooking together is one of the deepest ways to appreciate another culture — and it produces a delicious reward.

❤️ Key message: All cultures deserve respect. Curiosity about different ways of life is a sign of intelligence and kindness — not a sign that your own culture is less important.

Parent Guide — Unit 2: World Cultures

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Why cultural education at age 4–5: Children who are exposed to diverse cultures early develop empathy, flexibility of thinking, and resistance to stereotyping. This is not "political" — it is cognitive development. The brain's ability to categorize and generalize, if not given diverse inputs, will generalize from too little data and produce prejudice. Diverse cultural education is cognitive hygiene.

  1. Connect the five cultures to real books: "The Name Jar" (Korean-American), "Last Stop on Market Street" (urban American), "Drum Dream Girl" (Cuban-Chinese-African).
  2. Explore your own family's cultural background with the same depth and celebration given to other cultures.
  3. When your child makes a stereotyping generalization, gently redirect: "That's one way some people do it. There are lots of different ways. Let's find out more."
  4. Watch cultural celebration videos together — YouTube has beautiful footage of Diwali fireworks, Día de los Muertos processions, and Japanese cherry blossom festivals.

Assessment — Unit 2

🌐 Culture Explorer Check (10 min)

Q1. Ask your child to present their culture poster. "Tell me three things you learned about this culture."
Q2. "What is one food from a different culture you've tried or would like to try?"
Q3. "How do people in Japan greet each other? How do we greet each other? Both are respectful — why?"
Q4. "What is one thing all cultures have in common, no matter where they are?" (Music, stories, food, family, celebration — any of these is a great answer.)

📗 Unit 3 — Reading a First Chapter Book

Lesson for Kids

📖 About Chapter Books

A chapter book is different from a picture book. It is longer. It has chapters — each one a small piece of a bigger story. You don't finish it in one sitting. You stop, wonder what comes next, and come back again.

Reading a chapter book is like watching a TV series: each chapter ends at an exciting moment, and you can't wait to find out what happens next.

Good chapter books for first readers: "Elephant & Piggie" by Mo Willems, "Frog and Toad Together" by Arnold Lobel, "Magic Tree House #1" by Mary Pope Osborne, "Mercy Watson to the Rescue" by Kate DiCamillo.

How to Read a Chapter Book Together

  1. Before starting: Read the title, look at the cover. Ask: "What do you think this book will be about? Who do you think the main character is?"
  2. Each day — one chapter: Read one chapter together. Stop after each chapter and ask one question before putting the book down.
  3. Daily comprehension check: Use the four question levels — recall, sequence, infer, predict — rotating a different type each day.
  4. Keep a reading journal: After each chapter, the child draws one scene or writes/dictates one sentence about what happened.
  5. Finishing the book: Celebrate! Ask: "What was your favorite part? What would you change? Would you want to read another book about these characters?"

Reading Journal Prompts (one per chapter)

DayPromptLevel
1"Who are the main characters? What do you already know about them?"Recall
2"What happened in this chapter? Tell me in order."Sequence
3"Why did [character] do that? What were they feeling?"Infer
4"What do you think will happen in the next chapter?"Predict
5"What is the problem in the story? Has it been solved yet?"Theme

Parent Guide — Unit 3: Chapter Books

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Why chapter books are different: Chapter books require working memory (remembering what happened three days ago), inference (understanding characters' motivations), and sustained engagement over multiple sessions. These are the literacy skills tested all the way through high school and college. Building them early, through enjoyable chapter books, is one of the highest-leverage investments in a child's academic future.

  1. Even if your child cannot read the chapter book independently yet — that is fine. Read it aloud to them and have them follow along. The comprehension skills are the same.
  2. Make the daily reading a ritual: same time, same cozy place. Anticipation builds engagement.
  3. If your child asks to keep reading past one chapter — celebrate and let them. Never punish reading enthusiasm with a rigid schedule.
  4. After finishing, visit the library and let the child choose the next book entirely on their own. Ownership of reading choices creates lifelong readers.

Assessment — Unit 3

📗 Book Club Check (10 min, after finishing the book)

Q1. "Tell me the story from beginning to end in your own words." — Retelling tests comprehensive understanding.
Q2. "Who was your favorite character and why?" — Tests character analysis.
Q3. "What was the main problem? How was it solved?" — Tests plot comprehension.
Q4. "If you could add one more chapter, what would happen?" — Creative extension.

✍️ Unit 4 — Writing a Paragraph

Lesson for Kids

📖 What Is a Paragraph?

A paragraph is a group of sentences that all talk about the same idea. Think of it like a team — every player is on the same team, playing for the same goal.

A paragraph has three parts: a topic sentence that tells the main idea, detail sentences that explain or support it, and a closing sentence that wraps it up.

The Paragraph Frame

Topic Sentence (What is the paragraph about?)

Dogs make wonderful pets.

Detail Sentence 1 (First reason/example)

They are loyal and always happy to see you.

Detail Sentence 2 (Second reason/example)

Dogs can also learn tricks and help keep you safe.

Detail Sentence 3 (Third reason/example)

Playing with a dog is great exercise for kids.

Closing Sentence (Wrap it up)

That is why dogs are my favorite animals.

Activity 1 — My First Paragraph

Choose a topic your child loves (a pet, a food, a place, a season). Help them fill in the paragraph frame by dictating their answers. You write it down. Then they copy it, or write it themselves if ready. Read it aloud together. "You just wrote a real paragraph!"

Activity 2 — Paragraph Sort

Write 5 sentences on strips of paper (one topic sentence, three details, one closing). Mix them up. Ask the child to sort them into the right order. Discuss how they knew which was the topic sentence.

✅ Paragraph writing milestone: This is one of the most important academic skills of elementary school. Every essay, report, and research paper from 1st grade through college is built from paragraphs. The habit of "topic → details → close" is the backbone of all expository writing.

Parent Guide — Unit 4: Writing

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

The dictation-to-writing bridge: Most 4–5 year olds have ideas far more sophisticated than their physical writing ability allows. The bridge is dictation: you scribe what they say, then they copy (or trace) it. As fine motor skills catch up over the next year, they take over more of the physical writing. Separate the cognitive task (composing) from the physical task (handwriting) at this stage.

  1. Always affirm the ideas before commenting on spelling or neatness: "What a great topic sentence! You really explained your main idea clearly."
  2. Expand from 3 detail sentences only when the child finds 3 easy. Some children will want to write 5 or 6 detail sentences — celebrate and let them.
  3. Connect to the chapter book in Unit 3: each chapter could be described in a paragraph — great cross-unit practice.
  4. Display all written paragraphs — a wall of their writing builds pride and motivation.

Assessment — Unit 4

✍️ Writing Check (10 min)

Q1. Ask the child to write a paragraph on a topic of their choice. Observe: Is there a topic sentence? Are the details related to the topic? Is there a closing?
Q2. Give 5 scrambled sentences. Ask the child to identify the topic sentence first.
Q3. Ask: "What does a topic sentence do? Why does every paragraph need one?"
Q4. Read a paragraph aloud. Ask: "Is there a sentence here that doesn't belong? Which one?" Include one off-topic sentence in the paragraph.

✖️ Unit 5 — Multiplication Foundations

Lesson for Kids

📖 Story Time: The Fast Way to Add

Lily had 3 bags of apples. Each bag had 4 apples. "How many apples do I have?" she asked.

She could add: 4 + 4 + 4 = 12. Or she could think: "3 groups of 4 = 12!" Both answers are 12. But the second way is faster — it's multiplication.

"Multiplication," said her dad, "is just a fast way to add equal groups. 3 times 4 means 3 groups of 4."

Equal Groups

GroupsItems EachRepeated AdditionTotal

3 groups4 each4 + 4 + 412

Activity — Arrays with Stickers

Make an array: stick stickers in neat rows and columns. 3 rows of 5 = 15 stickers. Count by rows (add 5 three times) and by columns (add 3 five times) — same answer! This introduces the commutative property of multiplication visually.

✅ Goal at this stage: Understand multiplication as equal groups — NOT memorizing times tables. Tables come in 2nd–3rd grade. The concept is what matters now.

Parent Guide — Unit 5: Multiplication

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Concept before computation: Children who understand what multiplication MEANS (equal groups, arrays, repeated addition) master the times tables dramatically faster than children who are drilled on tables before understanding. This unit plants the conceptual seed. The formal tables harvest it — in about 2 years.

  1. Point out equal groups in real life: eggs in a carton (2 rows of 6), wheels on cars (4 per car × several cars), cookies on a baking sheet.
  2. Use the word "groups" every time: "How many groups? How many in each group? How many altogether?"
  3. Skip counting (2, 4, 6, 8... / 5, 10, 15, 20...) is the oral version of multiplication — practice it as a daily song or chant.
  4. The sticker array activity can be extended: make a 10×10 grid on paper — the child has just built the entire multiplication table visually.

Assessment — Unit 5

✖️ Equal Groups Check (10 min)

Q1. Place 3 cups with 4 blocks each. Ask: "How many groups? How many in each? How many altogether?"
Q2. Ask: "What addition problem matches 4 groups of 3?" (3+3+3+3)
Q3. Show an array of 2 rows × 5 columns. Ask: "How many stickers? How do you know without counting every one?"
Q4. Skip count by 2s to 20 and by 5s to 50 — clap on each number.

🍕 Unit 6 — Fractions: Equal Parts

Lesson for Kids

📖 Story Time: The Fairest Share

Sam and his sister had one pizza to share. "We need to cut it into EQUAL pieces," said Mom. "Equal means the same size — no sneaky big piece for you, Sam!"

She cut it into 2 equal pieces. "Each of you gets one-HALF. Written as a fraction: 1/2."

When Grandma joined them, they cut it into 4 equal pieces. "Now each person gets one-QUARTER — one-FOURTH. Written: 1/4."

"Fractions are just equal sharing," said Mom. "The bottom number says how many equal pieces total. The top number says how many pieces you have."

Fractions at a Glance

FractionNameMeaningReal-Life Example
1/2One-half1 piece out of 2 equal piecesHalf a sandwich, half an apple
1/4One-quarter / One-fourth1 piece out of 4 equal piecesQuarter of a pizza, 25¢ coin
1/3One-third1 piece out of 3 equal piecesA third of a granola bar shared three ways

Activity — Fraction Folding

Fold a square piece of paper in half — unfold and count: 2 equal parts → 1/2. Fold again for 4 equal parts → 1/4. Color one part. Write the fraction. Repeat with a circle (fold into thirds with slightly more effort) → 1/3.

⚠️ Key rule: Fractions only work with EQUAL parts. If the pieces are different sizes, it is not a fraction. This distinction is critical and often skipped.

Parent Guide — Unit 6: Fractions

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Why fractions matter so early: Fractions are the #1 stumbling block in upper-elementary math. Children who are introduced to the equal-sharing concept of fractions in early childhood consistently outperform peers who first encounter fractions as abstract notation in 3rd grade.

  1. Use food constantly: share crackers, cut fruit, divide playdough. The fairness angle ("equal pieces!") is inherently motivating to children this age.
  2. Emphasize that the bottom number (denominator) names how many equal pieces the whole is divided into. The top number (numerator) counts how many you have.
  3. Connect to halving/doubling in cooking: "If we use half a cup of sugar, how much is that?"
  4. Connect to time: half-past from Unit 6 of Book 4 — the clock face is a circle divided into equal parts. A child who reads "half past" has already internalized 1/2 spatially.

Assessment — Unit 6

🍕 Fraction Check (10 min)

Q1. Show a circle cut into 4 equal parts. Ask: "What fraction is one piece? Two pieces?"
Q2. Show two rectangles — one correctly halved, one with unequal halves. Ask: "Which one shows a real half? Why?"
Q3. "If 3 children share one banana bread equally, what fraction does each child get?"
Q4. Ask: "Which is more — one-half or one-quarter? How do you know?" Use food or folded paper to verify.
