Welcome to Book 2: My Amazing Body & Healthy Habits! — the second chapter in your child's home-schooling journey. Having discovered colors, shapes, numbers, feelings, and nature in Book 1, we now turn inward: exploring the incredible machine each child carries everywhere — their own body.

This book teaches children to be proud of and responsible for their bodies through hands-on sensory play, food exploration, healthy routines, and movement. We also complete the alphabet (N–Z) and push counting all the way to 20.

★How to Use This Book

💡 Tip from Book 1: One unit per week works well. But this book has many sensory activities — give your child extra time to explore freely. Learning at age 4 should feel like play, not school.
  1. Gather materials before the lesson (blindfold, food samples, mirror, toothbrush for demos).
  2. Read the story aloud with your child snuggled in — physical closeness activates calm, attentive learning.
  3. Do activities with your child, not for them — hand over control whenever safe to do so.
  4. Read the Parent Guide to understand the developmental "why" behind each activity.
  5. Run the assessment as a game — never as a test they could fail.
⚠️ Food allergy awareness: Units 3 and parts of Unit 1 involve tasting foods. Review ingredient safety for your child before those activities.

★Learning Goals

By the end of Book 2, your child will be growing toward:

  • Naming all five senses and their corresponding sense organs
  • Identifying major body parts (at least 10) and their basic functions
  • Sorting foods into healthy vs. treat categories
  • Independently performing at least 3 daily hygiene routines

👁️ Unit 1 — The Five Senses

Lesson for Kids

📖 Story Time: The Superpower Senses

Maya woke up and her five superpower senses switched on all at once.

Her eyes opened and she SAW the golden sunlight on the curtains. Her ears HEARD birds singing outside. She SMELLED her dad making pancakes with her nose.

She ran downstairs and TOUCHED the soft dog on the couch — its fur was warm and fluffy. Then she TASTED a blueberry — sweet and a little tangy — with her tongue.

"I used ALL FIVE senses before breakfast!" she told her dad, very proudly.

He laughed. "Your senses are your brain's messengers. They tell your brain everything happening in the world around you. Pretty amazing, right?"

Maya nodded, closed her eyes, and listened. She could hear the spatula scraping, the birds, the hum of the fridge. "The world is SO full of things to sense!"

Activity 1 — Sense Safari Bag

Fill a paper bag with 5 mystery items (a cotton ball, a pinecone, a small bell, a piece of orange peel, a square of velvet fabric). Have your child reach in without looking, pull out one item, and describe it using as many senses as possible before revealing it.

Activity 2 — Blindfold Taste Test

Prepare 5 small pieces of food with distinct tastes: a lemon slice (sour), a pinch of salt (salty), a grape (sweet), a pretzel (salty/crunchy), a tiny piece of dark chocolate (bitter/sweet). Blindfold your child. Let them taste each one and guess what it is.

✅ Vocabulary to build: sweet, sour, salty, bitter, crunchy, smooth, rough, loud, soft, bright, dark, fragrant, stinky.

Activity 3 — Sound Map

Sit very still outside (or by an open window) for 2 minutes. Have your child draw a simple map of what they HEAR — a circle for birds, a squiggle for cars, a dot for a dog bark. This builds focused listening and spatial awareness together.

👃 SmellNoseSniff 5 spice jars blind — can you name them?

Parent Guide — Unit 1: Five Senses

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Why the senses matter at age 4: Sensory processing is the foundation of all learning. Children who can accurately describe sensory experiences develop richer vocabulary, better reading comprehension (they can visualize text), and stronger scientific observation skills. Sensory play also regulates the nervous system — it is calming and focusing.

How to conduct the lesson:

  1. Introduce one sense per day — slow and deep beats broad and shallow.
  2. Name sensory experiences constantly in everyday life: "That blanket feels so soft — what else feels soft?"
  3. Avoid over-scaffolding the taste test — let the child struggle to identify foods. The struggle IS the learning.
  4. For children with sensory sensitivities: never force sensory exploration. Offer choices, keep it low-pressure, and celebrate any participation.
💡 Science extension: Ask "what would happen if you didn't have that sense?" to build empathy for people who are blind or deaf, and to deepen understanding of each sense's role.

Assessment — Unit 1: Five Senses

👁️ Sense Detective Game (10 minutes)

Q1. Point to your eye, ear, nose, tongue, and skin. Ask: "What do we do with each one? Which sense does it belong to?"
Q2. Play a quick round of "Which sense?" — describe something ("I can hear thunder — which sense is that?") and have the child name the sense.
Q3. Repeat the blindfold taste test with 3 foods. Did your child remember any from last time? Note improvement.
Q4. Ask: "If you couldn't see for one day, what would be hard? What could you still do?" — This builds both vocabulary and empathy.

🦴 Unit 2 — My Amazing Body

Lesson for Kids

📖 Story Time: What's Inside Me?

Carlos put his hand on his chest and felt it: thump-thump, thump-thump. "That's my HEART!" he said. "It never stops!"

He took a big breath in and puffed out his chest. "My LUNGS are filling up with air — like two balloons inside me!"

He ate his apple and said, "My STOMACH is going to turn this into energy for running and jumping!"

He touched his arm and felt something hard. "BONES! I have 206 of them! They're like my body's frame — without them I'd be like a wet noodle!"

He wiggled his fingers — ten of them! He wiggled his toes — ten more! He smiled into the mirror. "My body is the most amazing machine ever made," Carlos decided. "And it's MINE. I should take good care of it."

Activity 1 — Body Tracing

Roll out a large piece of butcher paper or tape several sheets together. Have your child lie down and trace their entire body outline. Then draw in the major body parts they know — head, hair, eyes, nose, mouth, ears, neck, shoulders, arms, hands, fingers, tummy, legs, feet, toes.

Activity 2 — Inside My Body

After the body tracing, use a red marker to draw the heart, a simple lung pair, and the stomach. Talk about what each organ does:

Organ/PartWhat It DoesFun Fact for Kids
❤️ HeartPumps blood everywhereBeats about 100,000 times every day!
🫁 LungsBring in air (oxygen) and push out wasteYou breathe about 20,000 times a day!
🧠 BrainControls everything — thinking, moving, feelingWeighs about 3 pounds but uses 20% of your energy!
🦴 BonesHold your body up and protect organsYou were born with 270 bones — some fuse together as you grow!
💪 MusclesMove your body when your brain sends signalsYou have over 600 muscles — even in your face!

Activity 3 — Heartbeat Science

Have your child rest quietly for 1 minute. Count heartbeats together by pressing two fingers gently to the inside of the wrist. Then do 20 jumping jacks. Count again. Ask: "Why is it beating faster now? What do you think our heart is doing?"

✅ Key vocabulary: heart, lungs, brain, bones, muscles, stomach, blood, oxygen, energy, organ.

Parent Guide — Unit 2: My Body

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Why body awareness matters at age 4: Children who understand and respect their bodies develop better physical health habits, stronger body autonomy (important for personal safety), and a foundation for health literacy throughout life. Body awareness also supports proprioception — knowing where your body is in space — which aids in reading, writing, and sports.

How to conduct the lesson:

  1. Use the body tracing as a centerpiece project — display it on the wall for the whole week and add to it.
  2. During physical activity, narrate body functions: "Feel your lungs working! Your heart is pumping blood to your legs right now!"
  3. Use the heartbeat activity as a micro-science experiment — prediction → test → observation → conclusion.
  4. Keep anatomical language accurate — use real names (heart, lungs, brain) not only slang ("tummy pump").
❤️ Body positivity note: Use language that celebrates what bodies CAN DO, not how they look. "Your legs are strong!" not "You're tall!" This builds intrinsic confidence rather than appearance-focused self-worth.

Assessment — Unit 2: My Body

🦴 Body Parts Game (10 minutes)

Q1. Play Simon Says using body parts: "Simon says touch your elbow! Touch your knee!" — Note which parts they know instantly.
Q2. Point to the body tracing poster. Ask: "Where is the heart? What does it do? Can you feel yours beating?"
Q3. Ask: "What happens to your heart when you run fast? Why do you think that is?"
Q4. Ask: "If your bone broke, what part of your body would be hurt? What would you not be able to do?" — Assesses understanding of function.

🥦 Unit 3 — Healthy Foods & My Plate

Lesson for Kids

📖 Story Time: The Rainbow Lunch

Lily's grandmother always said: "Eat the rainbow, little one."

Lily didn't understand at first — rainbows were in the sky, not in a lunchbox!

But then Grandma packed her lunch: red tomatoes, orange carrots, yellow corn, green broccoli, and blueberries that were almost purple. It WAS a rainbow!

"Different colors mean different vitamins," Grandma explained. "The red in tomatoes helps your heart. The orange in carrots helps your eyes. The green in broccoli makes your muscles strong!"

Lily ate every color. She felt strong and full and happy. "My body is like a car," she said. "Good food is the best fuel!"

Grandma smiled. "And sometimes we put a little treat in the tank too — cookies are fine sometimes, but broccoli is what makes the engine really GO."

Activity 1 — My Healthy Plate

Draw a large circle on paper (a dinner plate). Divide it into sections. Have your child draw or cut pictures from magazines to fill the plate with fruits, vegetables, grains, proteins, and dairy.

🥕 Fruits & Vegetables (half the plate!)

Apples, bananas, carrots, peas, broccoli, berries — eat every color of the rainbow!

🌾 Grains

Bread, rice, pasta, oatmeal, cereal — give you energy to play all day!

🥩 Protein

Chicken, beans, eggs, fish, tofu, nuts — build your muscles and repair your body!

🥛 Dairy

Milk, cheese, yogurt — strong bones and teeth need calcium every day!

Activity 2 — Healthy vs. Treat Sort

Print or draw pictures of 12 foods (6 healthy, 6 treats). Have your child sort them into two groups on the table. Discuss: "Can we eat treats? Yes! But how often? Sometimes. Can we eat vegetables every day? Yes!"

Activity 3 — Rainbow Smoothie

Make a smoothie together: a handful of spinach (green, but hidden by the fruit!), frozen mango (orange/yellow), blueberries (purple/blue), banana (yellow), and milk. Blend and drink. Ask: "How many food groups did we use? How many rainbow colors?"

⚠️ Important: Avoid framing any food as "bad" — this can create unhealthy relationships with food. Instead use: everyday foods vs. sometimes foods.

Parent Guide — Unit 3: Healthy Foods

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Why food education matters at age 4: Food preferences are heavily shaped by ages 2–6. Children who are exposed to a wide variety of foods (even if they don't always eat them) during this window develop more adventurous palates and better nutrition habits as adults. The goal is exposure, not consumption.

How to conduct the lesson:

  1. Involve your child in food preparation — washing vegetables, tearing lettuce, stirring. Children eat what they help make.
  2. Model adventurous eating yourself — children learn food attitudes from parents first.
  3. Use the "one bite rule" gently: "You just need to try one bite. You don't have to like it." Never force eating.
  4. Connect food to function: "Carrots have beta-carotene which helps your eyes see in the dark — like a superpower!"
💡 USDA MyPlate is the official US nutrition guide for kids. The key message for 4-year-olds: half the plate should be fruits and vegetables. Visit myplate.gov for free resources and kid-friendly visuals.

Assessment — Unit 3: Healthy Foods

🥦 Food Explorer Check (5–10 minutes)

Q1. Lay out 6 real or picture foods. Ask: "Which ones help your body grow strong? Which are sometimes treats?"
Q2. Ask: "What did you eat for breakfast today? Can you name one healthy thing in it?"
Q3. Ask: "What food helps your bones? What food gives you energy to run?" (Dairy → bones; grains/fruit → energy.)
Q4. Do a quick "rainbow check" — point to a fruit or vegetable for as many rainbow colors as the child can name.

🪥 Unit 4 — Healthy Habits & Hygiene

Lesson for Kids

📖 Story Time: Ben and the Germ Busters

Ben's mom had a superpower: she could see germs. (Okay, not really — but she knew where they hid!)

"Germs," she explained, "are tiny, tiny creatures — too small to see. They live on our hands, on surfaces, and in our mouths. Some germs make us sick. But we have five Germ Buster tools."

Germ Buster #1: Wash your hands — especially before eating, after the bathroom, and after touching animals. Use soap, sing Happy Birthday twice — 20 seconds!

Germ Buster #2: Brush your teeth — morning and night. Two minutes each time. Germs in your mouth make cavities!

Germ Buster #3: Cover your cough — into your elbow, not your hand. That way germs don't spread to others.

Germ Buster #4: Get enough sleep — your body fixes itself while you sleep. 10–12 hours for ages 4–5!

Germ Buster #5: Drink water — your body is 60% water. Drink 5–6 cups a day to keep everything working!

"If I do all five things every day, am I a Germ Buster?" Ben asked. "The BEST kind," said his mom.

Activity 1 — Handwashing Demo

Sprinkle a tiny bit of glitter on your child's hands — this represents germs. Then try to wash the glitter off with just water (hard!), then with soap (much easier!). The visual impact of glitter-as-germs is unforgettable.

Activity 2 — Healthy Habits Chart

Create a weekly chart with 5 rows (wash hands, brush teeth, drink water, sleep enough, cover coughs) and 7 columns (Mon–Sun). Each day your child completes a habit, they put a sticker in the box. At the end of the week, count the stickers together.

Activity 3 — Two-Minute Tooth Brush

Stand together at the mirror and brush teeth for the full 2 minutes. Use a timer or play a 2-minute song. Show the child how to brush all surfaces — front, back, and top of each tooth.

✅ Hygiene routines to master by age 5: Handwashing independently, 2-minute toothbrushing (with supervision), covering coughs/sneezes, using a tissue, putting dirty clothes in a hamper.

Parent Guide — Unit 4: Hygiene

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Why hygiene habits matter at age 4: Habits formed before age 5 often persist for life. Handwashing alone reduces respiratory illnesses by 20% in children. Building hygiene routines now prevents far more illness than any medicine — and the independence it creates builds self-confidence.

How to conduct the lesson:

  1. Do hygiene routines WITH your child — parallel modeling is more powerful than instruction.
  2. Use the Healthy Habits Chart for at least 3 weeks — habit formation takes 21+ repetitions.
  3. Make handwashing a non-negotiable routine anchor: always before meals, always after the bathroom. Consistency eliminates resistance over time.
  4. Explain the "why" without fear: "Germs can make you feel sick and miss fun things. Washing hands is the best way to stop them."
💡 Sleep tip: The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends 10–13 hours of sleep for ages 3–5 (including naps). A consistent bedtime is the single biggest predictor of healthy sleep at this age.

Assessment — Unit 4: Hygiene

🪥 Germ Buster Check (5 minutes)

Q1. Ask: "Can you name the 5 things Germ Busters do every day?" Prompt if needed — you're checking recall, not independent memory yet.
Q2. Watch your child wash their hands. Did they use soap? Did they scrub for 20 seconds? Provide gentle coaching, not criticism.
Q3. Ask: "What do you do when you need to cough? Show me." — Observe elbow-cover technique.
Q4. Count the stickers on the Healthy Habits Chart together. Celebrate the total. Ask: "Which habit was the easiest? Which was hardest to remember?"

🔤 Unit 5 — Letters N through Z

Lesson for Kids

📖 Story Time: The Last Letters Arrive!

The letters A through M had been waiting and waiting. "Where are the rest of us?" they asked.

Then, marching down the road, came the second half of the alphabet!

N led the way — Night, Nest, Noodles! O was round like an orange. P hopped by like a puppy. Q wore a crown — Queen! R roared like a rocket.

S slithered in like a snake. T tip-toed by on a tightrope. U was upside down — Umbrella! V made a V-shape with two fingers — Victory! W was a wonderful watermelon.

X made the tricky /ks/ sound — like in fox and box! Y yawned like a yak. And last but never least, Z zoomed in on a zipline — Zzzzzzzip!

All 26 letters cheered together: "Now you know ALL of us! You know the WHOLE alphabet!"

Activity 1 — Complete the Alphabet Wall

If you made an alphabet wall display in Book 1 (A–M), now add N–Z. Each letter gets its own card with a picture of the word. Arrange them in a long strip or arc on the wall — say the whole alphabet pointing to each letter once a day.

Activity 2 — Alphabet Song with Full Alphabet

Sing the traditional ABC song pointing to each letter. Then try the "slow version" where you pause at each letter and say a word that starts with it: "A… apple! B… ball! C… cat!" all the way to Z.

Activity 3 — My Name is Made of Letters!

Write your child's full name in large letters. Identify each letter, say its name and sound, find it elsewhere on the alphabet wall. For each letter in their name, think of another word that starts the same way.

Parent Guide — Unit 5: Letters N–Z

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Where we are in literacy development: By the end of Unit 5, your child should be able to recognize all 26 uppercase letters and associate each with its primary sound. This is the literacy milestone known as "alphabetic principle" — the understanding that letters represent sounds. This is the direct gateway to reading.

How to conduct the lesson:

  1. Review A–M letters from Book 1 briefly before introducing N–Z — spaced repetition strengthens memory.
  2. Prioritize letters in your child's own name — they are the most motivating and memorable.
  3. Note: X is unusual — /ks/ at the end of words (fox, box) vs. /z/ in some words (xylophone). For age 4, just say "X says /ks/ like in fox."
  4. Q almost always pairs with U — point this out and make it a fun rule: "Q never goes anywhere without its friend U!"
💡 Reading readiness check: Can your child: (1) say the alphabet in order, (2) identify all 26 letters randomly shown, (3) give the sound for each letter? If yes to all three, they are ready to begin simple CVC word work (cat, pig, sun) — a preview of Book 3!

Assessment — Unit 5: Letters N–Z

🔤 Full Alphabet Check (10–15 minutes, two sittings)

Q1. Flash all 26 letter cards in random order. For each, ask: "What letter is this?" and "What sound does it make?" Record results.
Q2. Say a sound (e.g., /s/, /w/, /z/) and ask the child to name a word that starts with that sound.
Q3. Open any children's book to a page. Ask the child to point to all the letters they know. Count them.
Q4. Ask your child to write (or trace) the letters of their first name without looking. Celebrate every letter attempt — the process matters far more than the result.

🔢 Unit 6 — Numbers 11 through 20

Lesson for Kids

📖 Story Time: The Teen Numbers Team

Numbers 1 through 10 had always been best friends. But one day, bigger numbers arrived in town!

"We're the TEEN NUMBERS!" they announced. "We're made of a ten AND some extras!"

Eleven said: "I'm 10 and 1 more!" Twelve said: "I'm 10 and 2 more!" Thirteen: "10 and 3!" All the way to Nineteen: "10 and 9!"

And then came Twenty — the grand arrival! "I'm TWO tens!" said Twenty proudly. "The very first number with TWO groups of ten!"

The original numbers were amazed. "So bigger numbers are just made of smaller numbers put together?"

"Exactly!" said Twenty. "That's the secret of all mathematics. You already know everything you need to go further — you just have to see how numbers build on each other!"

Activity 1 — Teen Number Building

Use two colors of blocks or counters. One color = tens, the other = ones. To build 14: place one "ten block" and four "one blocks" side by side. Say: "1 ten and 4 ones = fourteen!" Repeat for all teen numbers.

Activity 2 — Number Line Walk (1–20)

Extend the floor number line from Book 1 (1–10) all the way to 20. Jump, hop, or tiptoe to called numbers. Practice counting on: "Start at 12 — count on 3 more. 13, 14, 15! You're at 15!"

Activity 3 — More Than / Less Than

Place two groups of objects on the table. Ask: "Which group has MORE? Which has LESS? How do you know?" Build up to comparing numbers in the teens: "Is 16 more than 12? Let's find them on our number line!"

💡 Early addition preview: When your child is confident with 11–20, introduce the first addition concept: "If you have 10 strawberries and I give you 3 more — how many now?" Use objects, not just symbols.

Parent Guide — Unit 6: Numbers 11–20

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Why teen numbers are tricky: In English, eleven and twelve break the pattern (they don't say "one-ten" and "two-ten" like Chinese or Japanese, which makes those languages easier for early math!). Thirteen through nineteen reverse the digit order in speech (thirteen = 3+10, but we say the 3 first). This is genuinely confusing and requires extra practice.

How to conduct the lesson:

  1. Always connect teen numbers to "10 and some more" using physical objects before using written numerals.
  2. The number line (floor version) is invaluable — spatial representation of numbers supports all future math.
  3. Practice skip counting by 2s and 5s informally: counting stairs by 2s, counting toes by 5s.
  4. Introduce the symbols < (less than) and > (greater than) informally — point to them on paper, use hands to show the "mouth eating the bigger number."
⚠️ Don't rush to addition with symbols (+, =) yet. Concrete understanding (objects) must come before representational (pictures) before abstract (symbols). This sequence is the foundation of Singapore Math and most research-based math curricula.

Assessment — Unit 6: Numbers 11–20

🔢 Number Ninja Check (10 minutes)

Q1. Count out 17 small objects together. Ask: "How many are there?" Watch for one-to-one correspondence and cardinality.
Q2. Show number cards 11–20 in random order. Ask: "What number is this?" for each.
Q3. Put 13 blocks in one group and 17 in another. Ask: "Which group has more? How do you know?"
Q4. Say: "You have 10 grapes. I give you 5 more. How many do you have now?" Let them use fingers or objects — mental math comes much later.
