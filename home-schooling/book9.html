🧩 Unit 1 — Logic & Reasoning

Lesson for Kids

📖 Story Time: The Detective's Mind

A good detective doesn't guess — she thinks. She looks at the clues, finds the pattern, and reasons to the answer.

"Mud on the shoes," said young detective Priya. "It rained this morning — so this person was outside this morning. The mud is on the LEFT shoe only — so they stepped in one puddle. The puddle is at the park gate. THEREFORE: this person was at the park this morning."

Logic is reasoning from what you know to what you can conclude. If it rains, then the ground gets wet. If the ground is wet, then someone walking outside would get muddy shoes. Priya knew the "if-then" chain — and followed it all the way to the answer.

"You can use this kind of thinking for anything," her mentor told her. "Math, science, arguments, decisions. A logical mind is the most powerful tool you'll ever own."

Logical Thinking Types

TypeWhat It IsExample
Cause & EffectOne event causes anotherIt rained (cause) → the ground is wet (effect)
If-Then ReasoningLogical consequence of a conditionIf all birds have feathers AND a penguin is a bird → THEN a penguin has feathers
AnalogyRelationship between two pairsCat is to kitten as dog is to ___? (puppy)
SequencingEvents in logical orderSeed → sprout → plant → flower → fruit → seed

Activity 1 — Cause and Effect Chains

Write 6 events on cards (e.g., "it snowed" / "school closed" / "children played outside" / "came in wet and cold" / "had hot cocoa" / "fell asleep early"). Have the child arrange them in a cause-effect chain and explain each link.

Activity 2 — Analogy Puzzles

Present analogies verbally: "A glove is to a hand as a sock is to a ___?" (foot) / "A bird is to flying as a fish is to ___?" (swimming) / "A crayon is to drawing as a pen is to ___?" (writing). Start simple, increase complexity as confidence grows.

Parent Guide — Unit 1: Logic

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Why logical thinking at age 4–5: Analogies and cause-effect reasoning are among the strongest predictors of academic achievement — they appear in every standardized test (SAT analogies, reading comprehension cause-effect questions, scientific reasoning). Building these thinking patterns early, in a play-based context, is one of the highest-leverage investments in a child's cognitive future.

  1. Ask cause-and-effect questions constantly in daily life: "Why is the ice melting? What will happen if we leave the door open? Why is your friend crying?"
  2. Analogy puzzles can be scaled from concrete (cat:kitten) to abstract (fast:slow as warm:cold) — keep them at the child's challenge edge.
  3. Model "if-then" thinking aloud: "If I want to be on time, then I need to leave in 10 minutes. If we run out of milk, then we can't make pancakes — so let me check first."
  4. Connect to reading: after every story or non-fiction passage, ask: "What caused this event? What were the effects?"

Assessment — Unit 1

🧩 Logic Check (10 min)

Q1. Present cause-effect: "Maria didn't water her plant for two weeks. What happened? Why?"
Q2. Analogy: "Cold is to winter as hot is to ___?" / "Day is to sun as night is to ___?"
Q3. If-then: "If all mammals breathe air, and a whale is a mammal — what do we know about whales?"
Q4. Sequence: Give 5 events from a story out of order. Ask the child to arrange them correctly and explain why each goes where it does.

🔧 Unit 2 — Design Thinking

Lesson for Kids

📖 Story Time: The Bridge Problem

The toy animals couldn't cross the stream in the sandpit. "They need a bridge," said Kai.

Before Kai started building, his dad asked him five questions:

"Who needs this bridge?" The animals — especially the heavy elephant. "What's the problem exactly?" No way to cross without getting wet. "What ideas do you have?" Popsicle sticks, cardboard, a flat stone, twisted paper. "Which will you try first?" Popsicle sticks! "How will you know if it works?" The elephant won't fall in.

Kai built, tested — the elephant fell in. He redesigned, tested again — it held! "That process," said his dad, "is exactly how engineers design everything: the bridges you drive over, the phone in my pocket, the rockets we send to Mars."

Kai stared at his popsicle stick bridge. "I'm an engineer," he said.

"You always were," said his dad.

Mini Design Challenge — The Egg Drop

Challenge: protect a raw egg from a drop of 2 feet using only: 5 cotton balls, 4 rubber bands, 1 sheet of paper, 10 cm of tape. Plan → build → test (drop onto a hard floor). Redesign if it breaks. This is engineering at its purest — and children find it thrilling.

Parent Guide — Unit 2: Design Thinking

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Design thinking is real-world intelligence: The Stanford d.school's design thinking framework is used by Apple, IDEO, Google, and NASA. Teaching it at age 4 is not premature — it is the age when creative problem-solving habits are most malleable. A child who learns to define problems before jumping to solutions has a cognitive advantage in every field.

  1. The most important habit: "define the problem before solving it." Most people skip Step 2 and jump to solutions. Slow the child down: "Wait — what exactly is the problem? Say it in one sentence."
  2. Celebrate failed prototypes: "Your first try broke the egg! That's GREAT information. What did you learn? How will you change your design?" Failure is data, not defeat.
  3. The egg drop can be adapted: wrap a grape to survive being stepped on, build the tallest free-standing tower from 20 spaghetti strands and 1 marshmallow, design a container to keep ice cream cold for 10 minutes.
  4. Connect to adult work: "When doctors design a new medicine, they use these same 5 steps. When architects design a building. When programmers write software."

Assessment — Unit 2

🔧 Design Thinking Check (10 min)

Q1. Ask: "What are the 5 steps of design thinking? What happens in each one?"
Q2. Present a problem: "Your baby cousin always loses her pacifier. How would you design a solution?" Walk through all 5 steps.
Q3. Ask: "Your first egg drop design didn't work. What do you do next — give up, or try again differently?" — Tests persistence and growth mindset.
Q4. Ask: "Why is it important to DEFINE the problem before you start building?"

🏗️ Unit 3 — STEAM Projects

Lesson for Kids

📖 You Are Already a Scientist, Engineer, and Artist

STEAM stands for Science, Technology, Engineering, Art, and Mathematics. But it's not really about those words — it's about the way of thinking those fields share: observe, question, hypothesize, build, test, improve, and create.

Every time you built a block tower and wondered why it fell — that was engineering. Every time you mixed colors and noticed what happened — that was science. Every time you drew a picture to show what you felt — that was art. You have been doing STEAM your whole life.

Now we're going to do it on purpose.

Four STEAM Projects

ProjectMaterialsChallengeLearning
🌉 Popsicle Bridge20 popsicle sticks, craft glueBuild a bridge that holds 5 pennies without collapsingStructural engineering, triangles as strong shapes
✈️ Paper Airplane Design3 sheets of paper, rulerDesign the airplane that flies farthest — test 3 designsAerodynamics, scientific method, data collection
🌱 Mini GardenCup, soil, 3 seeds, waterGrow 3 plants with different watering amounts — compare growthVariables, plant biology, measurement over time
💡 Simple CircuitBattery, LED, wires (circuit kit or 9V battery + 1.5V LED)Light the LED; add a switch to turn it on/offElectricity, open/closed circuits, technology

Project Documentation Template

For each project, the child fills out a simple record:

  1. My Question: What am I trying to find out or build?
  2. My Plan: Draw or describe what you will do.
  3. What Happened: Draw or describe the result.
  4. What I Changed: What would you do differently next time?
  5. What I Learned: One sentence about the most important thing you discovered.
🔬 The scientific method in child-friendly language: Ask → Predict → Test → Observe → Conclude. These five words, repeated across every project, become a permanent mental habit.

Parent Guide — Unit 3: STEAM Projects

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Why project-based learning: Project-based learning (PBL) produces deeper, more transferable knowledge than traditional instruction because it requires children to USE knowledge, not just receive it. A child who builds a bridge understands triangular bracing in a way no lecture can produce.

  1. Let the child lead — resist fixing or improving their design unless safety is an issue. A collapsing bridge they built is more educational than a standing bridge you helped them build.
  2. The paper airplane experiment is an ideal introduction to controlled experiments: change only ONE variable at a time (wing shape, paper weight, fold depth), measure the same way each time, record the results.
  3. The simple circuit project may require a basic circuit kit (widely available, ~$10) or a 9V battery with alligator clips and an LED. Check online for "simple circuit for kids" for safe, clear instructions.
  4. The mini garden variable experiment (different watering amounts) is a genuine experiment with a control and variables — acknowledge this: "You just designed your first controlled experiment!"

Assessment — Unit 3

🏗️ STEAM Project Review

Q1. Review the project documentation for one project. Ask: "What was your question? What happened? What did you learn?"
Q2. Ask: "Which project was hardest? What made it hard? How did you keep going?"
Q3. Ask: "What would you change if you did the bridge/airplane/garden project again?" — Tests reflective learning.
Q4. Ask: "Can you explain how your circuit works? What happens when you open the switch?" — Tests conceptual understanding of electricity.

🔍 Unit 4 — Research Skills

Lesson for Kids

📖 How to Find Out Anything

Imagine you could answer any question in the world. You can — if you know how to research.

Research has four steps: Ask a good question (specific, not too broad). Find a reliable source (a book, a trusted website, an expert). Take Notes — write down only the most important facts, in your own words. Share — tell what you learned, and say where you found it.

"A question without a source is just an opinion," said the librarian. "A question with a source becomes knowledge. And knowledge shared becomes wisdom."

Research Process for Young Learners

  1. Choose a topic you love: dolphins, volcanoes, dinosaurs, space, butterflies — passion drives research.
  2. Write your question: Not "tell me about dolphins" but "How do dolphins communicate with each other?" Specific questions get specific answers.
  3. Find a source: A non-fiction library book, a trusted children's encyclopedia (World Book, Britannica Kids), or a reliable website (National Geographic Kids, NASA for Kids, Smithsonian Kids).
  4. Take notes: Write or draw 3–5 key facts. Use your own words — don't copy whole sentences.
  5. Share what you learned: Tell a family member, draw a poster, or write a short paragraph. Always name your source: "I learned this from a book called ___."

Activity — My First Research Project

Choose one animal, planet, or historical figure your child is fascinated by. Research it together using one book and one trusted website. Take notes on a simple template (3 facts, 1 surprising thing, 1 question you still have). Create a 1-page illustrated report titled "Everything I Know About [Topic]."

✅ Critical thinking extension: Find the same topic in two different sources. Ask: "Do both say the same things? Is there anything different? Which do you believe more? Why?" This is the beginning of source evaluation — a skill universities desperately wish all students had.

Parent Guide — Unit 4: Research

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Research literacy in the information age: A child who can formulate a specific question, find a reliable source, evaluate information, and cite it honestly is equipped for the information age in a way that most adults are not. These skills are the antidote to misinformation and intellectual passivity.

  1. Model research in your own life: "I don't know — let me look that up. I'll use a book/website I trust." Children who see adults engage in active research become researchers themselves.
  2. The library is the single greatest resource for this unit. A children's librarian can recommend exactly the right books at the child's reading level on any topic.
  3. Teach the difference between "I think" (opinion) and "I read that" or "According to ___" (cited fact). This simple distinction, established now, prevents plagiarism and intellectual dishonesty throughout their academic life.
  4. The "1 question you still have" note-taking prompt is the most important one — curiosity after research is the sign of a real scholar. Pursue those remaining questions together.

Assessment — Unit 4

🔍 Research Check (10 min)

Q1. Ask: "What is your research question? How is it specific?"
Q2. Ask the child to share 3 facts from their research. Ask: "How do you know this is true? Where did you find it?"
Q3. Ask: "What is the most surprising thing you learned? What question do you still have?"
Q4. Ask: "If someone told you something and you weren't sure if it was true, what would you do?" — Tests research mindset.

📐 Unit 5 — Geometry

Lesson for Kids

📖 Shapes in Three Dimensions

In Book 1, we learned flat shapes — circles, squares, triangles. But the world is three-dimensional! A circle drawn on paper is 2D. A ball is a SPHERE — a 3D circle. A square is 2D. A box is a CUBE — a 3D square in all directions.

Symmetry is another kind of geometry: a shape has symmetry when you can fold it in half and both sides match exactly. A butterfly is symmetric. A human face is almost symmetric. A circle has infinite lines of symmetry — every fold through the center works!

Area is how much surface a shape covers. We can count area by counting how many equal squares fit inside a shape. A 3×4 rectangle holds 12 squares — that's an area of 12 square units.

2D vs 3D Shapes

2D Shape3D VersionReal ExampleFaces / Edges / Corners
CircleSphereBall, orange, marble1 curved surface
SquareCubeDice, sugar cube, box6 faces, 12 edges, 8 corners
RectangleRectangular prismBrick, book, tissue box6 faces, 12 edges, 8 corners
TriangleTriangular pyramidPyramid, tent, party hat4 faces, 6 edges, 4 corners
CircleCylinderSoup can, toilet paper roll2 circular faces, 1 curved surface

Activity 1 — 3D Shape Hunt

Carry a list of 3D shapes around the house or grocery store. Find one real object for each shape: sphere (orange), cube (dice), cylinder (can), rectangular prism (cereal box), cone (party hat or funnel). Photograph or draw each one.

Activity 2 — Symmetry Butterflies

Fold a piece of paper in half. Put drops of paint on one half. Fold and press. Unfold: a perfectly symmetric butterfly pattern! Discuss: "Is this symmetric? Where is the line of symmetry?"

Activity 3 — Area Counting on Graph Paper

Draw shapes on graph paper. Count the squares inside. A 4×3 rectangle: count rows (4 rows of 3) = 12 square units. Make an irregular shape — count the squares inside, including half-squares. This is the foundation of calculus thinking, done with crayons.

Parent Guide — Unit 5: Geometry

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Geometry is everywhere: Architecture, art, engineering, biology, physics — all use geometric thinking. Children with strong spatial geometry skills consistently perform better in science, technology, engineering, and art. The investment in geometry at age 4–5 pays off across all STEAM fields.

  1. Use the correct vocabulary: face (flat surface), edge (where faces meet), vertex/corner (where edges meet). Children love technical vocabulary when it's connected to objects they're handling.
  2. The 3D shape hunt makes geometry a real-world scavenger game — take it to the supermarket, the park, or any building.
  3. Symmetry connects to art (mandalas, patterns), science (bilateral symmetry in animals), and mathematics (reflections, transformations in higher geometry).
  4. Area counting on graph paper is the conceptual foundation for area formulas (length × width = area) — which the child will derive themselves in a year or two. Let them count squares now; the formula will feel obvious later.

Assessment — Unit 5

📐 Geometry Check (10 min)

Q1. Hold up a soup can. Ask: "What 3D shape is this? How many faces does it have? What shape are its faces?"
Q2. Show pictures of 6 objects. Ask: "Which 3D shape is each one?"
Q3. Draw 4 shapes on paper (heart, rectangle, circle, irregular blob). Ask: "Which ones have a line of symmetry? Can you draw the line?"
Q4. Draw a 4×3 rectangle on graph paper. Ask: "How many square units fit inside?"

📊 Unit 6 — Data & Graphs

Lesson for Kids

📖 Story Time: The Class Pet Survey

The class wanted a pet, but everyone wanted something different. Instead of arguing, Maya said: "Let's collect DATA!"

She asked every classmate: "What pet do you want?" She made a tally mark for each vote. When she counted them: dogs had 8 votes, cats had 6, fish had 4, hamsters had 2.

She drew a bar graph — a picture of the numbers. Now everyone could SEE the answer: dogs were most popular!

"Data," said her teacher, "is information collected carefully and organized clearly. Scientists use it. Businesses use it. Governments use it. And now YOU use it."

Maya felt powerful. She had turned a problem (arguments) into information (data) into a picture (graph) into a decision (dogs!). "I want to do this with everything," she said.

Activity 1 — Family Favorite Survey

Survey your household (or 5–10 friends/relatives via text): favorite color, favorite food, or favorite season. Record with tally marks. Count tallies. Make a bar graph on graph paper — one bar per answer, height = number of votes. Read the graph: "Which was most popular? How many more votes did the winner get than the runner-up?"

Activity 2 — Pictograph

Make the same data into a pictograph — each vote is represented by a small drawing of the item (a tiny paw print for dogs, a tiny fish for fish). This is more engaging visually and builds the concept that different graph types show the same data different ways.

Parent Guide — Unit 6: Data

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent/Educator Guide

Data literacy is 21st-century essential: The ability to collect, organize, read, and interpret data is one of the most important skills for navigating the modern world. Every news story, election result, medical study, and business decision is presented in data form. Children who learn to read graphs critically are better citizens and better thinkers in every field.

  1. Survey questions can be anything the child is genuinely curious about: favorite book, bedtime hour, number of siblings. Real curiosity produces real engagement.
  2. Teach graph-reading explicitly: "Which bar is tallest? Which is shortest? How many more did [X] get than [Y]?"
  3. Connect to the weather reporter activity from Book 5, Unit 2 — the weather chart they made IS a data collection and graph. Point this out: "You've been doing data science all along!"
  4. Introduce the concept of "misleading graphs" — a very short bar looks smaller even if the numbers are similar. Critical reading of graphs is as important as creating them.

Assessment — Unit 6

📊 Data & Graph Check (10 min)

Q1. Show the completed bar graph. Ask: "Which had the most votes? Which had the fewest? How many total votes were cast?"
Q2. Ask: "How many MORE votes did dogs get than hamsters?" — Combining graph reading with subtraction.
Q3. Give a set of tally marks. Ask the child to count each group and tell you the total.
Q4. Ask: "If you could ask one survey question to your whole school, what would it be? What answers do you predict you'd get?" — Creative data thinking.
