Part one

How to homeschool right

Good homeschooling isn't about replicating a classroom in your dining room. It's five decisions, made deliberately, revisited every year as your kids and your life change.

Legal footing

Start with your state department of education, not a curriculum catalog. Requirements range from a simple letter of intent to annual standardized testing or portfolio review by a certified teacher. A handful of states require nothing beyond enrolling under an umbrella or private-school statute. Get this in writing before you buy a single workbook — it's the one step that can't be retrofitted.

Pick a method, not a brand

Most families land on one of five approaches, often blended: classical (grammar–logic–rhetoric stages, heavy on primary texts), unit studies (one theme taught across every subject), online academy (a virtual school with set courses and a teacher of record), unschooling (child-led, interest-driven learning), and eclectic (best-of-everything, assembled by the parent). Choose based on how your specific kid learns, not what's trending in a homeschool group.

Build a rhythm

A daily block schedule beats an hour-by-hour timetable — homeschooling routinely covers a day's material in 2–4 focused hours. Anchor the day around two or three fixed points (morning basket, lunch, one outside activity) and let subjects flex around them. Review the rhythm every quarter; what worked for a 7-year-old in September rarely fits the same kid by spring.

Keep real records

Log attendance, keep a work sample or portfolio per subject per year, and — starting in early high school — build an actual transcript with course titles, credit hours, and a grading scale. Colleges and even future employers care less about the format than about consistency. This record is also the paper trail states with reporting requirements will ask for.

Design for community

Socialization isn't a homeschool weakness to manage — it's a scheduling problem to solve. Co-ops, community sports leagues, park days, library programs, and part-time dual enrollment all do real work here. Aim for a standing weekly commitment outside the house by the end of month one, not "eventually."

Part two

Your first six weeks, in order

This sequence matters — each step depends on the one before it.

  1. 01

    Withdraw formally

    If your child is enrolled in public school, submit a written withdrawal notice — most states require this before homeschool status is legally recognized.

  2. 02

    File your notice of intent

    Send whatever letter, form, or umbrella-school enrollment your state requires. Keep a dated copy for your own records.

  3. 03

    Choose your method and curriculum

    Pick one approach for the year. Resist buying a full year of five different curricula before you've taught a single week.

  4. 04

    Set up your records system

    A simple spreadsheet or planner for attendance and work samples, started on day one, is far easier than reconstructing a year in June.

  5. 05

    Book one outside activity

    A co-op, sport, or class with a fixed weekly slot, locked in before the "real" schedule starts.

  6. 06

    Run a two-week trial

    Treat the first fortnight as a pilot, not a commitment. Adjust the schedule, not the child, once you see where the friction actually is.

Part three

Tax credits & financial help

This is the part families ask about most — and the part most likely to change. Here's where things actually stand.

Federal — no direct credit

There is still no federal tax credit or deduction specifically for everyday homeschool costs like curriculum, textbooks, or supplies — the IRS treats them as personal expenses, the same as any other cost of raising a child. What did change for 2026: families can now withdraw up to $20,000 per student per year from a 529 savings plan tax-free for K–12 expenses, up from $10,000, and the list of qualifying costs was widened to include curriculum, tutoring, testing fees, and dual-enrollment courses — a meaningful indirect benefit even without a credit. Separately, a new federal scholarship tax credit lets donors get a federal credit for gifts to scholarship-granting organizations; as of mid-2026 a majority of states have opted in, and some of those scholarships can be used toward homeschool expenses where state law allows.

Before you file

Programs, caps, and eligibility rules shift year to year, and some states tie ESA participation to giving up certain homeschool exemptions. Keep every receipt, confirm your state's current rules directly with its department of revenue or education, and — especially the first year you use an ESA or 529 withdrawal for homeschooling — loop in a CPA. Five mistakes that sink a first year

Skipping the paperwork

Filing intent late — or not at all — can put a family out of compliance without anyone noticing until it's a problem.

Copying a school day

Six hours of desk work at home isn't more rigorous — it's just longer. Homeschool efficiency is the point.

Buying before testing

A full curriculum set purchased in July, before a single lesson, is the most common source of buyer's remorse.

Letting records slide

Reconstructing a transcript from memory in senior year is avoidable — logging as you go isn't optional.

Frequently asked

Do I need a teaching certificate to homeschool? ›

In the large majority of states, no. A few states set a minimum parent education level (such as a high school diploma), and a small number require teacher involvement only in specific oversight models. Check your state's exact requirement before assuming either way.

Is homeschooling legal in every state? ›

Yes, all 50 states permit it. What differs enormously is the oversight level — from a one-time notice to annual assessments — so "legal" doesn't mean "identical."

Does the Child Tax Credit change if I homeschool? ›

No. The Child Tax Credit is based on having a qualifying dependent, not on how the child is educated — homeschooling neither helps nor hurts your eligibility.

Will homeschooling hurt college admissions? ›

Generally no, provided you can present a clear transcript, course descriptions, and — where useful — outside validation like standardized test scores or dual-enrollment credit. Most colleges now have an established homeschool admissions process.

Is a $10,000 federal homeschool tax credit coming? ›

Not currently. This claim circulates online periodically, but as of 2026 there is no such federal credit enacted or scheduled. The real 2026 change is the expanded 529 withdrawal limit and the new federal scholarship tax credit described above.
